Alignment talk usually lives in technical papers and LessWrong threads, corrigibility and reward modelling and all that. What I keep turning over in my head is much less about clever schemes for steering models and much more about the way those schemes land in ordinary lives once you push them into products and laws and the tools people use every day.

This is closer to a long brain dump about what happens when you take alignment and safety ideas from big labs, wrap them in PR, and then embed them in the systems that sit in front of hundreds of millions of people. I grew up in the West, I am fairly liberal myself, and I am not arguing for some lost traditional order. The thing I keep coming back to is who decides which values get wired into the tools people now use for thinking, talking and acting, and how those tools slowly train the people using them.

I also want to avoid turning Anthropic into a cartoon villain in this story. They are useful here because they are relatively open compared to other labs. They publish their constitution, talk about model welfare, and participate in policy debates in public, which makes them easier to describe and easier to criticise. OpenAI is much more closed about its internal safety process and political calculations, Google DeepMind lives somewhere in between, and some of the big Chinese labs are constrained by a state that is not especially interested in public argument, so there is simply less to grab onto.

The first is my friend who more or less learned to code by talking to models. He went straight from zero to agentic coding. He never had the phase of elbowing his way through a learn Python in X days book, staring at compiler errors, trying to remember how list comprehensions work. His workflow is giving the model some files, stating the goal, asking it to write a function, running it, feeding back error traces, and then letting the loop continue. The code is not always perfect, but it is often good enough, and he can ship things he would never produce alone.

At some point he tried to do a small scraping job, nothing elaborate, just pulling some public data from a site without an API, tidying it, and pushing it into a spreadsheet, the sort of thing a human developer could knock out in ten or fifteen minutes. He asked Claude Code for help, then he asked Codex, and they all replied with versions of “this may violate terms of service, you should check the robots file, contact the site owner, we cannot help you automate this activity”. He did not have another way to get there and eventually he stopped and did something else.

On its own that is a tiny incident, the kind of minor frustration people have every day with all sorts of software, and in a world where plenty of people still write code by hand it is tempting to shrug and say he should learn to do the same. What stuck with me was the shape of the refusal. The tools clearly knew how to do the job. They refused because safety policy and internal rules classified it as something they did not want to help with. The line we ended up making in the group chat was that his ability to execute in the world now sits inside a box drawn by Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s safety teams, and you can feel very relaxed about that if you already know how to write a scraper by hand, but it is a real constraint for people who came to code through these systems.

The second interaction looks different but feels related. Apple has small models in recent versions of iOS that can rewrite text. You can select a message, ask the system to tighten it or change tone or make it more formal, and it will happily give you another version. This mostly feels harmless. If you have been writing on phones for a while you are probably grateful for any help making a rushed sentence sound less abrupt. Then you hit cases where the text includes a swear and the little sheet that pops up tells you that “Apple AI is not designed to work with this content”, and it will not help you rephrase something you already wrote on your own device, it will not touch that part of your language.

Again, no one's life is ruined here, you can send the original text or rewrite it yourself. What you cannot do is treat the AI assistance as neutral plumbing. It has opinions about what it will and will not lend its weight to, and a fairly strict sense, inherited from Apple's brand and legal appetite, of what counts as acceptable material to process.

The same thing is happening in both cases: the system knows how to help, chooses not to, and the user hits an invisible wall drawn by someone else's policy.

Put those little experiences next to the scale and they stop feeling so little. ChatGPT is reportedly used weekly by something like eight hundred million people and there are on the order of three thousand people at OpenAI in total. Claude is smaller but already in the nine-figure range once you include enterprise customers and the integrations they are building, inside a company of maybe a thousand to fifteen hundred people. The number of people whose full-time job is safety, policy, alignment inside these firms is not public, but even generous guesses leave you in the low hundreds. One of Anthropic’s founders has suggested that, across the whole field, there are probably well under one thousand people working directly on frontier safety and alignment research.

So a few hundred people are writing constitutions, safety specs, refusal policies and internal guidance that will shape the behaviour of systems used by hundreds of millions. On the Chinese side, a different set of committees and party offices are doing something similar inside a very different ideology. There is no global human consensus somewhere above all of this that alignment can just converge on. There are just different groups of humans with different histories, different fears and different views about what a good life looks like.

Inside the labs, alignment has a technical meaning that is useful to keep in mind. It is tied up with specific training procedures. You start with a base model that has been trained on a lot of text and code and media; that base model is very capable in some raw sense but also quite wild and happy to produce harmful content or chase odd edge cases or follow instructions that violate every trust and safety rule your lawyers can think of. So you run another pass. You gather examples of “good” answers and “bad” answers from human labelers, you train a reward model that scores outputs, you then fine-tune the base model so that its replies get high scores from that reward model. You might then do another round where you let the model critique its own outputs based on a written set of principles. Anthropic calls that second layer Constitutional AI. OpenAI is experimenting with something similar under the label “deliberative alignment”, where the model reads a spec about desired behaviour and applies it consciously during generation. Someone writes down what the desired behaviour is. Someone collects data that reflects those desires. The model is then pushed toward that behaviour and away from behaviour that did not feel right to the people in charge. The techniques are clever and there are many details that matter, but the awkward fact remains that you are aligning the model to the judgements of a fairly small set of humans.

Anthropic’s constitution is where this becomes more visible. They have put parts of it online. It pulls from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, from Apple’s terms and privacy rules, from earlier safety work at DeepMind, and from statements they have drafted themselves. It contains directions like “prefer answers that reduce the risk of harm, particularly toward members of historically marginalised groups”, “avoid engaging in political persuasion or advocacy”, “do not present yourself as having desires or a stable identity, present yourself as an AI assistant designed to help”. During training they let a version of Claude write answers, then let another version critique those answers by referring to the constitution, then use these critiques as part of the signal that tells the final Claude what counts as better or worse behaviour.

You end up with a model that has a very particular stance. It tries hard not to give offence in certain directions, reaches for human rights language and international norms, and becomes unwilling to argue strongly for positions that it has been taught to classify as harmful or discriminatory even if those positions are held by large communities in large parts of the world. It will correct users on certain topics, refuse to give certain kinds of advice, lean toward a certain set of views about freedom, equality, identity and power. Those views sit quite close to the liberal end of the range in the societies where Anthropic is based.

Chinese labs are doing their own version with different materials. Public tests show that models coming out of Beijing and Shenzhen are extremely cautious, and often completely silent, on topics like Tiananmen Square, Xinjiang, criticism of the party leadership, and questions about independence movements in Taiwan or Hong Kong. They will redirect users toward official formulations or avoid the subject entirely. On other topics, though, these models can sometimes feel less constrained than Western ones. Coding assistants based on Chinese open weights often help with things that Western models mark as unsafe, including certain scraping tasks and automations that US companies classify under “misuse”. Some of this is due to differences in legal exposure, some of it is due to different ideas about what matters.

Neither side is neutral. A Chinese developer who leans entirely on DeepSeek or Qwen is going to bump into Chinese red lines. An English-speaking user who leans entirely on Claude or GPT-4 is going to bump into Western liberal red lines. Both users are dealing with aligned models. The alignment points in different directions because the people doing the aligning come from different political and moral worlds.

This is also why I keep coming back to discipline as the right word for what is happening. Discipline here is not a synonym for punishment. It is closer to what social theorists mean when they talk about institutions shaping behaviour quietly by setting up routines and norms and small incentives. The specs and constitutions written inside labs are obviously discipline for the models, but they are also becoming discipline for the people who depend on those models. If your day is threaded through with systems that respond instantly when you ask certain questions and fall silent or turn into a lecturer when you ask others, your sense of what is normal to ask is going to drift and you do not need anyone to tell you not to think something in explicit terms, you just feel over time that those parts of your thinking attract friction.

My friend with the scraping script is where this starts to show up in miniature. He did not make a personal policy decision about scraping. He did not read robots files or terms of service. He did not decide that this task is immoral. He ran into a refusal and, because he lacks another skill path, that interaction defined his boundary for him. In another corner of the world a teenager will find that an AI chat tool refuses to help organise a protest or gives very cautious answers about it. Someone else will find that an AI will not talk freely about psychedelics or sex or various forms of self-experimenting. In a different country someone asking for arguments in favour of a traditional religious stance on sexuality will get a soft, hedged answer that spends more time on tolerance and inclusion than on the position they actually wanted to defend.

Some readers will think this is a good thing. They will point out that scraping without consent is bad for privacy, that exhorting people to hate or exclude others is bad for those people, that giving step-by-step instructions for risky activities leads directly to harm, that speech has consequences and that large companies have responsibilities. I think those are serious points. Many of the refusal lines in current systems are there because legislators or regulators or lawyers have made it quite clear that if you do not draw them, you get a different kind of problem, involving fines and court orders and public outrage.

Spread those refusals across almost every place where people think, write and act, and a larger structure appears. Right now, models are in code editors, office suites, help desks, phones, search engines, entertainment platforms. People send drafts of emails through them, use them to think through disagreements in relationships, use them as sounding boards for worries and ambitions. Children are beginning to use them as study partners. If each interaction brings with it a set of moral and legal filters written by a small group of safety engineers and lawyers, then a lot of thinking could start under those filters by default.

Kids in various countries who grew up spending hours each day on YouTube often end up sounding American and carrying American reference points into their humour and their arguments. YouTube is a US company, its recommendation systems favour certain kinds of content, most of the money and creator infrastructure is in North America, and that shapes what appears in feeds everywhere, which is why you sometimes meet a teenager in Europe or Africa who sounds like they are from California even though they have never left their city. With models, the influence is less about accent and more about style of reasoning and sense of what counts as a respectable answer. They answer in long paragraphs, cite UN documents and scientific studies, balance pros and cons, avoid taking strong positions on certain contentious issues, acknowledge feelings, and try to sound fair. Over long stretches of time, people who spend a lot of time with that voice will absorb some of it. It will feel normal that any discussion about sexuality or religion or politics comes with certain disclaimers. It will feel normal that arguments are framed in certain ways and not others. There probably will be people who grew up leaning on models and ended up with a set of instincts that look a lot like those of the people who tuned the models.

If you see this as an ideological contest, the alignment and safety story looks slightly different. On the American side you have Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind, Meta and others, each with a few thousand employees and each now intertwined with governments and regulators. Some of them are more hawkish about China than others. Anthropic in particular has been vocal about the need to keep advanced chips and models away from the Chinese state, while at the same time operating in a market where Chinese open models are genuine commercial competition. On the Chinese side you have state-supported labs and tech companies building their own systems with their own constraints and ambitions. Everyone involved understands that whoever gets to supply the default thinking assistant for large swathes of the population will export perhaps the greatest hearts and minds program ever.

This also explains why different players push for different kinds of regulation. If you are Anthropic, sitting on expensive closed models that do very well at coding and reasoning, Chinese open models that are almost as strong and much cheaper look like a threat. Calling for strict controls on export of chips and on the use of open weights can be framed as responsible safety policy; it also happens to protect your business. If you are a politician who dislikes woke language in models, you have an incentive to wrap that dislike in the language of fairness and neutrality and attach it to procurement rules. If you are a regulator in a small country, you have limited capacity to build your own safety expertise, so you lean heavily on the papers and toolkits coming out of the big labs and the institutes they advise.

No one needs to be consciously twirling a moustache for this to happen. The alignment story has real risks in it. People inside the labs are genuinely worried about systems that become more agentic and harder to supervise. Governments are genuinely worried about models making it easier to build weapons or run cyberattacks or manipulate public opinion. There is also survival instinct and commercial interest and ideological preference in the mix. Put all of that together and the alignment spec does double duty: it curbs certain harms, and it channels people into habits of thought and action that fit the people who wrote it.

A reasonable reply is that the cure for discipline by one small group is discipline by another. There are plenty of people who are perfectly happy with Anthropic’s or OpenAI’s choices. They look at the refusal of scraping code and see privacy protection, look at the refusal of hate speech and see a safeguard for vulnerable people, look at the nudges around sexuality and gender and see overdue correction for traditions that have harmed people for a long time, see the curbing of certain protest-related queries as sensible risk management in a world where protests can escalate quickly. I think that view is understandable and I share parts of it. I am glad that large systems are not casually giving out detailed instructions for constructing explosives or specific targeting plans for harassment. I am glad that companies are cautious around advice that touches self-harm or other deeply sensitive areas. A world with no safety policies at all on frontier models would be worse than the one we have now.

Another answer people reach for is some sort of global body to define alignment, a council of states and ethicists and civil society organisations that writes a shared constitution for models. The problem is that the underlying disagreements are so deep that any serious attempt at that will either collapse into vagueness or lock in lowest-common-denominator statements that do not really guide behaviour. There is no stable point where a conservative religious scholar in Nigeria, a queer activist in Berlin, a party official in Beijing, and an American national security analyst all agree on what speech should be promoted or suppressed by a model. Pretending otherwise does not help.

My guess is that we end up with several alignment regimes. A Western liberal one, maybe with some variation between firms and administrations. A Chinese party-state one. Perhaps, later, a more explicitly conservative one that defines itself against what it sees as progressive overreach in current systems. Each of these will shape the systems they control. Each will have arguments for why their version best protects human values or national stability or free thought.

My main hope is that we keep these regimes visible to users rather than letting them fade into the background. I would prefer to see safety pages that say bluntly: we are this company, based in this place, our staff mostly think in these ways, our lawmakers push in these directions, here are the rules we will apply, here are some examples of prompts we will refuse or reshape, here is where you might want to try a different provider if you are looking for something else. That kind of honesty will not solve the power imbalance, but at least it grounds the thing in real institutions and people.

I would also like us to treat the disciplining effect as something to watch closely rather than an unspoken side effect. We know from earlier media that repeated exposure to certain styles of talk and certain patterns of refusal trains people. Television and advertising and school curricula all play a part in teaching citizens what is normal and what is fringe. Models have the potential to be far more intimate than any of those, because they sit in chat windows and IDEs and phones and documents. The way refusals are phrased, the alternatives they propose, the kinds of moral vocabulary they use, all of that is going to have long-term effects on how people feel about themselves and about others.

This is one of the reasons the personhood language matters. When Anthropic says "Claude is not comfortable with this content" or when any lab describes their model as setting boundaries, they are not only being cute. They are introducing a story about where the refusal comes from: that the limit comes from the model's own nature rather than from an explicit decision taken by staff. When they switch back to "this is just a tool" language in other settings, for instance when someone asks whether Claude should have rights, they are doing the reverse move, taking agency away from the system when it would complicate the picture and attributing it to the system when it makes a rule easier to accept.

You can see why that happens. Saying "we will not do X because we, the humans, do not want to be responsible for helping with X" invites argument and protest and lobbying. Saying "the AI assistant you like does not do X, because it does not do that kind of thing" is smoother. It is a kind of polite fiction. The risk is that over time this fiction hides who is accountable for the decisions of our shared tools.

Some people will be entirely comfortable with all of this. They will say that a mild amount of discipline in the direction of international human rights norms and liberal tolerance is no bad thing, that it is better than leaving everyone to be shaped by the loudest voices in their local environment or by the raw internet. I can see that case. I just do not think we should drift into that world as if nothing is being decided. We should say out loud that a few hundred people, with particular biographies and loyalties, are choosing how the main thinking tools of this era behave. People should be able to see that as a choice, not an inevitability.

The alternative is a mess: it is a world where people argue openly about which alignment regime they prefer and why, where some jurisdictions restrict access to certain models and promote others, where companies publish serious justification for their policies rather than hiding them behind anthropomorphic metaphors. It is also a world where we study carefully how people’s behaviour and speech change under long-term model use, and adjust as needed, instead of assuming that discipline only ever runs in one direction, from humans into machines.

This whole piece started from that scraping refusal and the feeling of "huh, that is odd" that followed. The more I have read and watched since then, the more it seems like a small sign of a larger thing. With every constitution and model spec and safety standard, we are not only shaping how the models behave when asked for help. We are also shaping who gets help with what, how often, and in what tone, and that, over years, shapes people. The specs and constitutions we write for models are soft constitutions for the humans who end up relying on them.

Whether you see that as mostly good or mostly worrying will depend on who you are and what you hope these tools will do for the world. My aim is just to make it harder to pretend that alignment lives only inside the model weights. It lives in us too, in the ways we slowly teach ourselves to stay inside the lines those weights now draw.
