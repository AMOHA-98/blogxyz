Conversations I keep having with friends about the West keep circling back to the same frustration. We talk about housing, trains, energy, defence, robots, supply chains, data centres, even basic public services, and then we hit this wall where everyone agrees the thing should exist, everyone can describe the thing in a powerpoint, and then the thing either takes ten years longer than it should or it never happens at all. It is tempting to treat that as a story about competence, or culture, or corruption, or some vague decline in “work ethic,” and I do not think any of those explanations are useless, but they also feel like ways of avoiding the real question, which is the question I want to sit with in this post. What does a society select for, year after year, when it gets very good at measuring and rewarding outcomes that are legible in money, and very bad at protecting the long, slow accumulation of physical capability that does not show up neatly on a quarterly chart.

The Chinese have language for this it comes up in official speeches and in the broader policy atmosphere, usually framed as a contrast between the “real economy” and the “virtual” or “fictitious” economy. In Mandarin you see 实体经济 versus 虚拟经济. The point is not subtle: the “real” side is factories, infrastructure, production, supply chains, exports, the physical system of making and moving things; the “virtual” side is finance froth, asset inflation, speculation, sectors that feel like money rotating on itself. Xi Jinping has stressed versions of this contrast in public remarks, and Chinese media and policy writing returns to it often, usually with a warning that capital should not drift too far away from the real economy and into the virtual one. Bloomberg, for example, reported as far back as 2017 on Xi emphasising the real economy as a foundation and scolding “fake data,” and there have been repeated warnings since then about finance and property crowding out manufacturing and technology. 

I am not bringing this up because I think China has discovered the one correct way to run a society. I bring it up because it captures, with unusual clarity, something the West struggles to say without sounding either conspiratorial or nostalgic: an economy can become extremely sophisticated at producing paper wealth, platform revenue, and financial returns, and still become less capable at producing physical capacity, resilience, and national power in the old sense.

That is the core of the argument I want to make: short-termism, combined with the talent magnets of finance and attention-based tech, has hollowed out the West’s ability to build in the world of atoms, and the reason this matters more now than it did a decade ago is that AI is forcing the software world into a collision with physical constraints, which turns “industrial capacity” from a political talking point into a binding variable.

There is a habit in western discourse where we treat the economy as a number, and then we treat any high number as proof that the system works. GDP up, markets up, valuations up, tax receipts up, therefore everything is fine. Meanwhile the actual experience of living in a place can degrade in ways that are not captured by the number: the trains are late, the housing is unaffordable, the grid is strained, the ports are congested, the procurement is broken, the infrastructure is old, the industrial base is thin, and if you try to build something large you find yourself in a maze of approvals, lawsuits, and political fights.

Money matters. Prices matter. Markets matter. The issue is that when you elevate one measurement system to the point where it becomes the only scoreboard that counts, you start selecting for people who are exceptionally good at winning on that scoreboard, even if the act of winning does not strengthen the physical layer underneath.

The most obvious example is finance. A large chunk of high-prestige finance is not about building new productive capacity directly; it is about competing inside markets that already exist, where the goal is to extract an edge, to price something a little faster than the next firm, to capture spread, to arbitrage information, to structure deals, to package risk, to engineer instruments, to optimise taxes, to improve return metrics. Some of this is socially useful. A lot of it is simply profitable. The marginal value of the marginal sophistication is not automatically the same thing as the marginal private return, and the further you push into speed races and complex optimisation, the harder it becomes to argue that the private return maps cleanly to broad social benefit.

The other obvious example is the attention economy, which is basically a machine for converting human time into revenue. The mechanics are well understood, so I will not explain them at length, but the incentive structure matters. A platform whose revenues are overwhelmingly advertising has a direct reason to maximise engagement, to maximise time on platform, to maximise the number of impressions and the quality of targeting, to drive behaviour that increases ad inventory. This incentive structure was probably a net-positive in the early era of the internet, because it subsidised distribution and access, and it made it possible for a lot of services to exist without direct payment. The problem is that we built the modern internet’s surface area on a business model that rewards behavioural manipulation, and then we acted surprised when the downstream consequences looked like a society being trained into short horizons, fragmented attention, and political polarisation.

That is the high-level picture, but it becomes more concrete when you look at what happens to people.

### The career ladder problem

A smart person choosing a career is responding to gradients: money, prestige, risk, stability, social proof, the recruiting machine, and also the availability of good ladders. If the strongest ladders in a society are ladders that pay you to play inside money systems and attention systems, then a lot of the society’s best talent will rationally climb those ladders, and the things that do not have comparable ladders will get the leftovers.

This is where offshoring enters the story. It is common to discuss offshoring as a macroeconomic trade story: manufacturing moved to cheaper labour markets, supply chains globalised, the US specialised in services and high-end design, and consumers benefited from lower prices. That story is true, but it has a second-order consequence that matters for long-run capacity: offshoring erodes the domestic industrial ladder. It does not just move jobs. It moves learning.

Manufacturing at scale is a form of embodied knowledge. It lives in production engineers, toolmakers, line managers, maintenance cultures, quality systems, supplier networks, the people who know which tolerances matter and which ones you can relax, the people who have seen every failure mode and know how to route around it. When an economy offshores enough of the routine practice of making things, it begins to lose the everyday competence that makes new industrial ventures easier. You can still build pockets of excellence, and the US does, but the thick middle becomes thin, and rebuilding the thick middle is not a matter of slogans. It is a matter of spending years living inside factories again.

Now put that next to the career ladder problem.

If you are an elite new graduate and you are deciding between a path that offers clear money and prestige in finance or big tech, and a path that offers a smaller salary, less glamour, and fewer obvious “fast feedback” wins in manufacturing, you are not just choosing between jobs instead you are choosing between two different kinds of life and two different kinds of risk. The salary differences matter a lot here, and they matter not only because of material comfort, but because they signal what the society values.

This is naturally what happens when elite institutions become pipelines for the industries that can monetise elite signals most aggressively.

Recruiting timelines have moved earlier. The pipeline into finance and consulting has become more formal and more intense. Princeton alumni reporting, to pick one prominent example because it captures the dynamic, describes how recruiting schedules push students into early decisions, and how a large share of Princeton seniors with jobs end up in finance and consulting as first destinations. If you speak to students at other elite schools, you hear similar patterns. Some of that is preference. Some of that is pressure. Some of it is simply that the most organised industries show up first and offer the clearest ladders.

When this becomes a stable pattern across decades, it shapes the entire talent distribution of a society. It also shapes what kinds of people end up in government, in industrial firms, in infrastructure, in the places where long-horizon coordination happens. It is difficult to run a long-term industrial project in a system where the strongest selection pressure pushes the most optimisation-minded people into domains where the feedback is fast and the pay is extreme.

At this point it is easy to slip into a kind of “people are greedy” narrative, and I do not think that narrative is very useful. The more interesting story is about horizons.

Industrial capacity is a long-horizon asset. The return is slow. The work is slow. The skill accumulation is slow. The failure modes are expensive. The learning curves are steep. The payoff is often political rather than purely financial: resilience, independence, geopolitical leverage, the ability to act under constraint.

Short-termism is the enemy of all of that, and it shows up in at least two places: corporate governance and democratic politics.

On the corporate side, the mechanism is straightforward. Public markets reward outcomes that can be measured and compared in short windows. There are some public firms that invest heavily and think long-term, and I am not trying to reduce everything to a caricature, but the pressure exists, and we know it exists because executives themselves have said so. There is interesting research, based on surveys and interviews, where many managers admit they are willing to cut long-term investment or sacrifice long-term value to meet short-term earnings targets. There is also research suggesting that public firms invest less than comparable private firms and are less responsive to investment opportunities, consistent with a world where market pressure discourages long-horizon spending. If you are rewarded for meeting quarterly expectations, and punished when you miss them, and if your stock price and your job security move with those expectations, then you become cautious about long-cycle bets that may pay off in five or ten years but could produce ugly numbers in the next quarter.

That is a problem for industrial capacity because industrial capacity often requires making ugly numbers on purpose. It requires capex. It requires years of investment before payoff. It requires building redundant supplier relationships. It requires training workforces. It requires tolerating waste and iteration. Those are all things that look bad in a short-term accounting sense if you do not have a system that protects them.

On the political side, the mechanism is different but the outcome is similar. Democracies have short election cycles. Even in countries with longer terms, the effective political horizon often collapses toward the next election, because politicians are selected for winning the next cycle, not for executing a twenty-year build plan. When political polarisation increases, the problem compounds, because policy becomes more reversible, and reversibility makes long-horizon projects riskier.

It is one thing to build a factory or a power plant in a stable policy environment where the rules are predictable. It is another thing to do so in a world where you cannot be sure what the regulatory regime will be in four years, what the tax incentives will be, what the trade regime will be, whether permitting will tighten or loosen, whether your sector will become a political football. Policy uncertainty becomes a tax on long projects.

Economists have tried to capture this with measures like the economic policy uncertainty index, and while any index has limitations, the general finding is consistent: higher uncertainty correlates with lower investment and weaker activity. One prominent paper by Baker, Bloom, and Davis lays out how they measure policy uncertainty and documents its association with economic outcomes, including investment.

If you want a more visceral illustration, look at how quickly executive actions can be reversed across administrations. In the US, a new president can rescind large numbers of previous executive orders on day one, and that has become a normal pattern. Executive Order 14148, issued on January 20, 2025, explicitly rescinded dozens of Biden-era presidential actions, which illustrates how rapidly policy direction can swing even without new legislation.

I am not making a partisan argument about whether those rescissions were good or bad. I am making a structural argument: if you are a firm trying to commit to long-horizon industrial projects, the ease of reversal is itself a risk factor. It pushes you toward projects with shorter payback periods, and it pushes you toward sectors where you can pivot quickly, which almost always means sectors that live more in bits than in atoms.

Now combine corporate short-termism and political short-termism, and you get a system that is structurally biased against long-horizon industrial build. The bias does not require anyone to explicitly hate manufacturing. It only requires the system to reward short-term wins more reliably than long-term capacity.

It is easy for Western observers to describe China’s focus on manufacturing and industrial capacity as “authoritarian planning,” and then stop thinking, because “authoritarian planning” is a category label that lets you outsource explanation to ideology. The more useful question is why the Chinese system and the Chinese elite discourse keep returning to real-economy strength as a core national objective, even as China becomes wealthier and more service-oriented. Some of it is historical. China’s modern legitimacy is strongly tied to material improvement, and the story of the last forty years has been industrial growth, exports, and rising living standards. Some of it is geopolitical. China faces a strategic environment where dependence on external supply chains is a vulnerability, and where technological containment is a real risk, so building domestic capability is not just an economic preference, it is a security posture. Some of it is the structure of state capacity itself: the Chinese government has more levers to coordinate and push industrial outcomes, and its political cycles do not reset direction in the same way.

There is also, as Dan Wang has emphasised, a different corporate orientation toward scale and market share. He has noted that many Chinese firms optimise for market share more than for high profits, which helps explain why certain Chinese industries become brutally competitive and low-margin, while still building enormous physical capacity and ecosystem depth.

That “market share first” instinct can look irrational through a pure Western shareholder-value lens, but it makes more sense if you treat scale itself as power, because scale buys learning curves, supplier control, bargaining power, and resilience. A firm that dominates a market and has the capacity to produce at scale can survive margin compression, can outlast competitors, can export aggressively, and can accumulate a base of physical competence that becomes hard to dislodge.

This is where the “measure in stuff rather than dollars” motif is useful, not as a literal claim that Chinese officials never look at money, but as a description of what gets celebrated and what becomes an input into decision-making. A famous illustration of this is the so-called “Li Keqiang index,” which became shorthand for the idea that some Chinese observers trusted physical proxies like electricity consumption and rail cargo more than headline GDP, because physical proxies are harder to fake. The very existence of that popular index, regardless of how seriously you take it analytically, signals something about what counts as reality in the discourse.

Once you see the contrast, it becomes hard to unsee it. In the West, we talk about market caps, earnings, multiples, and “value creation” in ways that often float free of physical output. In China, the language is more often about production, installations, throughput, exports, and industrial upgrading.

Again, this is not a moral endorsement. It is a different optimisation target, and optimisation targets shape societies.

What gets left behind, in this combined world, is the slow, unglamorous work of building physical capacity. If the best money and status ladders pull talent toward finance and feeds, and if political and corporate short-termism punishes long-horizon build, then the industrial layer is forced to operate with thinner talent, thinner institutional priority, thinner continuity.

You can see the result in the way the West often struggles with basic execution. Build times stretch. Costs balloon. Projects become politicised. The cognitive style of the elite becomes more lawyerly, more financial, more narrative-driven, and less operational. Dan Wang's "engineers versus lawyers" framing is provocative, but it is pointing at something real in the way societies allocate prestige.

The Fanduel transit story captures this dynamic in miniature. In September 2025, SEPTA's special service to the Eagles home opener had been cut amid a funding crisis, and Fanduel stepped in with a sponsorship to restore the Broad Street Line Sports Express. A gambling company temporarily filling a public transit gap so that people can get to a stadium and keep using the gambling platform. It is the virtual economy patching the real economy just enough to keep the extraction loop running, and the fact that this can happen without a national argument about priorities says something about where we are.

### Software brain

I want to talk about software brain: a cognitive crux that forms when your work takes place in environments with fast feedback, reversible decisions, cheap iteration, and clean metrics, and then gets exported, via culture, status, and capital, into domains where those assumptions do not hold.

Software environments teach you to expect progress to be a function of iteration speed. They teach you that most failures are recoverable, most mistakes can be rolled back, and most complexity can be tamed if you instrument enough of it. This is a powerful way to operate inside a digital domain. The physical world is not obligated to behave like a codebase. It has lead times that cannot be negotiated. It has constraints that do not yield to persuasion. It has materials that are scarce, and labour that is trained slowly, and supply chains that break in ways that are not captured by dashboards until the moment you are already failing. It has permitting regimes, safety regimes, maintenance realities, weather, geography, and the simple fact that a factory cannot be patched instantly without downtime and cost.

When people with a software brain talk about hard tech, you can sometimes hear the mismatch in the cadence of their expectations. They assume the problem is primarily the idea, or the model, or the capital, and that execution is an engineering sprint that can be solved with enough intensity. Then they collide with the kind of execution that is mostly logistics and patience and operational discipline, and the project slows down, and the narrative starts to sound like excuses, because the physical world’s constraints do not make for good storytelling.

This is where AI becomes interesting for reasons that go beyond hype.

AI is the first time in a long time that the most valuable part of the software economy is being forced into a scaling problem that is fundamentally physical. It is not that hyperscalers never built data centres before, or that tech companies never touched hardware; the shift is that, with AI, the physical layer is no longer background infrastructure that you can mostly treat as “someone else’s job.” It becomes the constraint that gates the frontier. If compute is the bottleneck, then power is the bottleneck. If power is the bottleneck, then grid interconnect and transformers and transmission are bottlenecks. If those are bottlenecks, then you are inside the world of utilities, permitting, construction, supply chain lead times, and hard operational reality.

The IEA recently estimated that data centres consumed about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption, and projects that data centre electricity use could roughly double by 2030 to around 945 TWh in its base case, with AI as a major driver. You can debate precise numbers, but the direction is the important part: we are pushing into a regime where the growth of AI is directly constrained by the ability of societies to build physical infrastructure quickly.

Reuters has reported that in the US, data centres could drive a large share of electricity demand growth through 2030, and that utility companies are receiving unprecedented power requests, with a meaningful fraction of planned projects facing delays due to grid constraints and infrastructure shortages. That is the collision; it is the point where software ambition meets the wall of physical lead times. I keep calling this a test because the outcome will teach us something about whether the Western system can still translate intelligence into physical throughput.

If the West cannot build enough power and enough data centres and enough transmission to support its own AI ambitions, then it does not matter how good the models are. The models will be gated by the ability to pour concrete, approve projects, move electrons, build supply chains, and operate real systems. If, on the other hand, the AI era forces a resurgence of operational seriousness, a return of prestige toward physical build, and a renewed capability to execute large infrastructure projects, then it could partially counteract the trends that have been hollowing out the industrial layer. That is why I find the data centre buildout interesting in a way that most AI discourse misses. It is a litmus test about whether a society that has become extremely good at bits can still do atoms when atoms become the binding variable again.

There is an obvious counterpoint that people reach for when you talk about Western inability to build, and that counterpoint is Elon Musk, usually via Tesla and SpaceX. Musk could have done what many other extremely successful tech people did, which is to stay in software, stay in finance, stay in platforms, stay in arenas where iteration is fast and outcomes are legible in money metrics, and instead he pushed into domains where the bottleneck is physical execution, manufacturing, supply chain, and operational risk.

Tesla and SpaceX are serious manufacturing and execution achievements, and they are also unusual in the modern American context, which is why they get cited so quickly. The fact that people can reach for one or two examples and everyone immediately knows what is being referenced is, by itself, a signal. It suggests that the “industrialist who chooses the pain” pattern is not the modal outcome of the Western elite system.

### Where this is going, and why it matters more in an AGI-shaped world

I think about AGI a lot, and I have tried to be careful about what I mean when I say it, because people use the term to mean everything from “chatbots with better reasoning” to “a system that can do nearly all cognitive work.” For the purpose of this post, I do not need to commit to a timeline or a precise definition, because the structural point holds under a wide range of futures.

Digital intelligence makes the digital world cheaper. Anything that can be done entirely in bits, or simulated entirely in bits, tends to accelerate when the tools improve, because iteration costs fall and the ability to search through solution spaces rises. That does not automatically mean the physical world accelerates at the same rate, because the physical world is gated by production, materials, energy, logistics, and coordination. If we get very strong systems for digital work, you can expect a lot of domains to move faster on the digital side: software production becomes more automated, digital design loops compress, research workflows accelerate, content production explodes, advertising and persuasion become more targeted and more efficient, recommendation systems become sharper, financial strategy generation becomes more sophisticated. The “virtual” side of the economy, the part that can be copied and scaled at near-zero marginal cost, will get even more extreme.

That creates a trap for societies that already over-reward the virtual side.

If the easiest way to deploy powerful digital intelligence is to make the attention machine more effective, and to make financial extraction more efficient, and to make gambling interfaces more compelling, then you get a world where the West takes the most powerful tool it has ever built and pours it into the same sinks that have already been draining civic and industrial capacity. The alternative is that strong AI turns the physical bottleneck into the main story, because the ability to deploy intelligence at scale becomes limited by power, compute, factories, robots, and supply chains. In that world, the societies that can build physical infrastructure quickly, reliably, and at scale have an advantage. This is why I do not think the after AGI story is automatically a story of instant physical abundance. The binding constraints remain physical for a long time, and those constraints are exactly the constraints that short-term, financialised societies struggle with.

In that sense, the most important question is not “how smart are the models,” but “how serious is the society about building the physical layer that turns intelligence into action.”

The narrow reason for hope is that AI might force a partial rebalancing of incentives, simply because it is turning physical build into the choke point for the most valuable part of the software economy. If the frontier is compute and power and data centre construction, then the people who can do operations, infrastructure, energy, robotics, manufacturing integration, and supply chain are no longer “old economy.” They are the ones who decide whether progress is possible. That could pull talent back. It could pull prestige back. It could pull capital back. It could force the Western system to relearn some of the operational habits it has allowed to erode, because the market reward for physical throughput becomes too large to ignore.

The broader reason to be worried is that the system’s default reaction to new tools is to financialise them, to platformise them, and to turn them into extraction engines, because those are the paths that fit the existing ladders and the existing short horizons. If we build a world where AI accelerates the attention machine, accelerates financial micro-optimisation, and accelerates gamblingification, then we get richer in the narrow sense and weaker in the deep sense. This is why I keep returning to the Chinese “real economy versus virtual economy” framing, even if I do not like the politics that often comes attached to it. The framing is correct about one thing: a society that lets the virtual side dominate for long enough starts to forget how to do the real work. It starts to confuse money with power. It starts to treat physical capacity as something you can always buy later, and then one day it discovers that you cannot buy later what you did not practice building for decades.

A society that only measures in dollars will eventually forget how to measure power.
