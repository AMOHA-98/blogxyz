Conversations I keep having with friends about the West keep revolving back to the same frustration. We talk about housing, trains, energy, defence, robots, supply chains, data centres, even basic public services, and then we hit this wall where everyone agrees the thing should exist, everyone can describe the thing in a PowerPoint, and then the thing either takes ten years longer than it should or it never happens at all. Competence, culture, corruption, some vague decline in “work ethic”: I keep hearing those explanations, and they each account for something. I still find myself coming back to the incentives that keep producing the same result. What does a society select for, year after year, when it gets very good at measuring and compensating outcomes that are legible in money, and very bad at protecting the long, slow accumulation of physical capability that does not show up neatly on a quarterly chart.

The Chinese have language for this. It comes up in official speeches and in the larger policy atmosphere, usually framed as a contrast between the “real economy” and the “virtual” or “fictitious” economy. In Mandarin, you see 实体经济 versus 虚拟经济. The contrast is not subtle: the “real” side is factories, infrastructure, production, supply chains, exports, the physical system of making and moving things; the “virtual” side is finance froth, asset inflation, speculation, sectors that feel like money rotating around itself. Xi Jinping has stressed versions of this contrast in public remarks, and Chinese media and policy writing return to it often, usually warning that capital should not drift too far from the real economy and into the virtual one. Bloomberg, for example, reported as far back as 2017 on Xi emphasising the real economy as a foundation, and there have been repeated warnings since then about finance and property crowding out manufacturing and technology.

The Chinese framing gives me language for something the West struggles to say without appearing either conspiratorial or nostalgic: an economy can become extremely sophisticated at producing paper wealth, platform revenue, and financial returns, and still become less capable at producing physical capacity, resilience, and national power in the old sense.

The argument is simple: short-termism, combined with the talent magnets of finance and attention-based tech, has hollowed out the West’s ability to build in the world of atoms. That matters more now because AI is forcing the software world into a collision with physical constraints, which turns industrial capacity from a political talking point into a hard limit.

Western discourse has a habit of treating the economy as a number, then treating any high number as proof the system works. GDP up, markets up, valuations up, tax receipts up, therefore everything is fine. Meanwhile the actual experience of living in a place can rot in ways the number barely notices: the trains are late, the housing is unaffordable, the grid is strained, the ports are congested, the procurement is broken, the infrastructure is old, the industrial base is thin, and if you try to build something large you find yourself in a maze of approvals, lawsuits, and political fights.

Money, prices, and markets tell us useful things about an economy. When they become the only scoreboard that counts, you start selecting for people who are exceptionally good at winning on that scoreboard, even when winning doesn't strengthen the physical layer underneath.

The most obvious example is finance, especially the high-end quantitative version that hoovers up mathematically gifted people and pays them to compete inside markets that already exist. A large chunk of high-prestige finance rewards extracting an edge within existing markets: pricing something a little faster than the next firm, capturing spread, arbitraging information, structuring deals, packaging risk, engineering instruments, optimising taxes, improving return metrics. Some of this is socially useful; a lot of it produces profits with little corresponding gain in productive capacity. The private reward for each extra layer of advancement is not the same thing as the public value of that sophistication, and the further you push into speed races and complex optimisation, the harder it becomes to argue that the private return maps cleanly to broad social benefit.

The other obvious example is the attention economy, which is basically a machine for converting human time into revenue. Big Tech built extraordinary infrastructure over the last twenty years. Much of it also serves a business model whose incentives are worth following through. A platform whose revenues are overwhelmingly advertising has a direct reason to maximise engagement, maximise time on platform, maximise impressions, improve targeting, and drive any behaviour that increases ad inventory. This was probably a net-positive in the early internet, because it subsidised distribution and access, and it made it possible for a lot of services to exist without direct payment. The problem is that we built the modern internet’s surface area on a business model that rewards behavioural manipulation, and then acted surprised when the downstream consequences looked like a society being trained into short horizons, fragmented attention, and political polarisation.

The career ladder problem

A smart person choosing a career responds to gradients: money, prestige, risk, stability, social proof, the recruiting machine, and the availability of good ladders. If the strongest ladders in a society are ladders that pay you to play inside money systems and attention systems, then a lot of the society’s best talent will rationally climb those ladders, and the things without comparable ladders will get the leftovers. This is the part I keep finding hard to shake: how many people who might have become great engineers, scientists, doctors, chemists, builders, or public servants did we route into ad auctions, feed ranking, quantitative trading, market making, tax optimisation, and internal growth dashboards because those were the pipelines that paid, recruited early, and knew exactly how to absorb them. How much of that choice reflected the availability of a good pipeline, and how much reflected where their minds could do the most?

Offshoring enters here. It is common to discuss it as a macroeconomic trade story: manufacturing moved to cheaper labour markets, supply chains globalised, the US specialised in services and high-end design, and consumers benefited from lower prices. That story is true, but it leaves out something that matters for long-run capacity: offshoring erodes the domestic industrial ladder. As the jobs move, the learning that happens around production moves with them.

Manufacturing at scale depends on tacit knowledge, the process knowledge people accumulate through years of making things. It lives in production engineers, toolmakers, line managers, maintenance cultures, quality systems, supplier networks, the people who know which tolerances matter and which ones you can relax, the people who have seen every failure mode and know how to route around it. When an economy offshores enough of the routine practice of making things, it begins to lose the everyday competence that makes new industrial ventures easier. You can still build pockets of excellence, and the US does, but the thick middle becomes thin, and rebuilding it means spending years living inside factories again, accumulating that knowledge through the work.

Now put that next to the career ladder problem.

If you are an elite new graduate and you are deciding between a path that offers clear money and prestige in finance or big tech, and a path that offers a smaller salary, less glamour, and fewer obvious “fast feedback” wins in manufacturing, that choice shapes the kind of life you can expect and the risks you will have to take. The salary differences affect your material comfort and tell you something about what society values.

This is what happens when elite institutions become pipelines for the industries that can monetise elite signals most aggressively.

Recruiting timelines have moved earlier. The pipeline into finance, consulting, and big tech has become more regulated and more intense. Princeton alumni reporting, to pick one prominent example because it captures the nature, describes how recruiting schedules push students into early decisions, and how a large share of Princeton seniors with jobs end up in finance and consulting as first destinations. If you speak to students at other elite schools, you hear similar patterns. Some of that is preference. Some of that is pressure. Some of it is simply that the most organised industries show up first and offer the clearest ladders.

When this becomes a stable pattern across decades, it shapes a society's entire talent distribution. It also determines what kinds of people end up in government, in industrial firms, in infrastructure, in the places where long-horizon coordination happens. It is hard to run a long-term industrial project in a system where the strongest selection pressure pushes the most optimisation-minded people into domains where the feedback is fast, the money is extreme, and many of the problems are, in the deepest sense, invented.

The time horizons of those careers matter too. People are responding to how long they will have to wait for their work to pay off.

Industrial capacity is a long-horizon asset. The return is slow. The work is slow. The skill accumulation is slow. The failure modes are expensive. The learning curves are steep. The payoff is often political rather than purely financial: resilience, independence, geopolitical leverage, the ability to act under constraint.

Short-termism is the enemy of all of that, and it shows up in at least two places: corporate governance and democratic politics.

On the corporate side, the story is simple. Public markets reward outcomes that can be measured and compared in short windows. Some public firms invest heavily and think long-term. Even so, executives themselves describe pressure to meet short-term targets at the expense of that investment. Interesting research based on surveys and interviews shows that many managers admit they are willing to cut long-term investment or sacrifice long-term value to meet short-term earnings targets. Research also suggests that public firms invest less than comparable private firms and are less responsive to investment opportunities, consistent with a world where market pressure discourages long-horizon spending. If you are rewarded for meeting quarterly expectations, and punished when you miss them, and if your stock price and your job security move with those expectations, then you become cautious about long-cycle bets that may pay off in five or ten years but could produce ugly numbers in the next quarter.

That is a problem for industrial capacity because industrial capacity often requires making ugly numbers on purpose. It requires capex. It requires years of investment before payoff. It requires building redundant supplier relationships. It requires training workforces. It requires tolerating waste and iteration. Those are all things that look bad in a short-term accounting sense if you do not have a system that protects them.

On the political side, the route is different but the destination is similar. Democracies have short election cycles. Even in countries with longer terms, the effective political horizon often collapses toward the next election, because a politician's ability to carry through a twenty-year build plan depends on repeatedly winning the next election. When political polarisation increases, the problem compounds, because policy becomes more reversible, and reversibility makes long-horizon projects riskier.

It is one thing to build a factory or a power plant in a stable policy environment where the rules are predictable. It is another thing to do so in an environment where you cannot be sure what the regulatory regime will be in four years, what the tax incentives will be, what the trade regime will be, whether permitting will tighten or loosen, whether your sector will become a political football. Policy uncertainty becomes a tax on long projects.

Economists have tried to capture this with measures like the economic policy uncertainty index, and while any index has limitations, the pattern is not surprising: higher uncertainty tends to go with lower investment and weaker activity. One prominent paper by Baker, Bloom, and Davis lays out how they measure policy uncertainty and documents its association with economic outcomes, including investment.

For a more visceral illustration, look at how quickly executive actions can be reversed across administrations. In the US, a new president can rescind large numbers of previous executive orders on day one, and that has become a normal pattern. Executive Order 14148, issued on January 20, 2025, explicitly rescinded dozens of Biden-era presidential actions, which illustrates how rapidly policy direction can swing even without new legislation.

For a firm trying to commit to long-horizon industrial projects, the ease of reversal is itself a risk factor, whatever the merits of the particular policies being reversed. It pushes you toward projects with shorter payback periods, and it pushes you toward sectors where you can adjust quickly, which almost always means sectors that live more in bits than in atoms.

Put corporate short-termism and political short-termism together, and you get a system that is biased against long-horizon industrial build. That bias develops whenever the system rewards short-term wins more reliably than long-term capacity, even among people who want manufacturing to succeed.

It is easy for Western observers to describe China’s focus on manufacturing and industrial capacity as “authoritarian planning,” and then stop thinking, because “authoritarian planning” is a category label that lets you outsource explanation to ideology. The more useful question is why the Chinese system and the Chinese elite discourse keep returning to real-economy strength as a core national objective, even as China becomes wealthier and more service-oriented. Some of it is historical. China’s modern legitimacy is strongly tied to material improvement, and the story of the last forty years has been industrial growth, exports, and increasing living standards. Some of it is geopolitical. China faces an environment in which dependence on external supply chains is a vulnerability, and where technological-related containment is a real risk, so building domestic capability is more than an economic preference; it is a security posture. Some of it is the structure of state capacity itself: the Chinese government has more levers to coordinate and push industrial outcomes, and its political cycles do not reset direction in the same way.

There is also, as Dan Wang has emphasised, a different corporate attitude toward scale and market share. He has noted that many Chinese firms optimise for market share more than for high profits, which helps explain why certain Chinese industries become brutally competitive and low-margin, while still building enormous physical capacity and ecosystem depth.

That “market share first” instinct can look irrational through a pure Western shareholder-value lens, but it makes more sense if you treat scale itself as power, because scale buys learning curves, supplier control, bargaining power, and toughness. A firm that dominates a market and can produce at scale can survive margin compression, outlast competitors, export aggressively, and build a base of physical competence that becomes hard to dislodge.

The emphasis on measuring in stuff tells you something about what gets celebrated and treated as real. Chinese officials watch financial numbers too; physical output has a particular standing in the way they describe progress. A famous illustration is the so-called “Li Keqiang index,” shorthand for the idea that some Chinese observers trusted physical proxies like electricity consumption and rail cargo more than headline GDP, partly because local officials had incentives to flatter the numbers above them. The very existence of that popular index, regardless of how seriously you take it analytically, signals something about what counts as reality in the discourse.

Once you see the contrast, it becomes hard to forget it. In the West, we talk about market caps, earnings, multiples, and “value creation” in ways that often float free of physical output. In China, the language proves more often about production, installations, throughput, exports, and industrial upgrading.

I find that choice of target revealing about how societies develop, even with my objections to the politics around it.

What gets left behind, in this combined world, is the slow, unglamorous work of building physical capacity. If the best money and status ladders pull talent toward finance and feeds, and if political and corporate short-termism punishes long-horizon build, then the industrial layer has to run on thinner talent, thinner institutional priority, thinner continuity.

You can see the result in the way the West frequently struggles with basic execution. Build times stretch. Costs balloon. Projects become politicised. The cognitive style of the elite becomes more lawyerly, more financial, more narrative-driven, and less operational. Dan Wang’s “engineers versus lawyers” framing is provocative, but it points to something real in how societies allocate prestige.

The Fanduel transit story captures this in miniature. In September 2025, SEPTA’s special service to the Eagles home opener had been cut amidst a funding crisis, and Fanduel stepped in with a sponsorship to restore the Broad Street Line Sports Express. A gambling company temporarily filling a public transit gap so that people can get to a stadium and keep using the gambling platform. It is the virtual economy patching the real economy just enough to keep the extraction loop running, and the fact that this can happen without a national argument about priorities says something about where we are.

Software brain

I want to talk about software brain: the mental habit that forms when your work takes place within environments featuring fast feedback, reversible decisions, cheap iteration, and clean metrics, and then gets exported, via culture, status, and capital, into domains where those assumptions do not hold.

Software environments teach you to expect progress to be a function of iteration speed. They teach you that most failures are recoverable, most mistakes can be rolled back, and most complexity can be tamed if you instrument enough of it. This is a powerful way to operate inside a digital domain. The physical world is not obligated to behave like a codebase. It has lead times that cannot be negotiated. It has constraints that do not yield to persuasion. It has materials that are scarce, labour that is trained slowly, and supply chains that break in ways that are not captured by dashboards until the moment you are already failing. It has permitting regimes, safety regimes, maintenance realities, weather, geography, and the simple fact that a factory cannot be patched instantly without downtime and cost.

When people with software brain talk about hard tech, you can sometimes hear the mismatch in the cadence of their expectations. They assume the problem is mainly the idea, or the model, or the capital, and that execution is an engineering sprint that can be solved with enough intensity. Then they collide with the kind of execution that is mostly logistics, patience, and operational discipline; the project slows down, and the narrative starts to sound like excuses, because the physical world’s constraints don't make for good storytelling.

AI becomes interesting here for reasons that go beyond hype.

AI is the first time in a long time that the most valuable part of the software economy is being forced into a fundamentally physical scaling problem. Hyperscalers have been building data centres and dealing with hardware for years. With AI, that physical work moves to the centre of what determines progress. Power and construction become problems the software side has to confront directly. If compute is the bottleneck, then power is the bottleneck. If power is the bottleneck, then grid interconnects, transformers and transmission are bottlenecks. If those are bottlenecks, then you are inside the world of utilities, permitting, construction, supply chain lead times, and hard operational reality.

The IEA recently estimated that data centres consumed about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption, and projects that data centre electricity use could roughly double by 2030 to around 945 TWh in its base case, with AI as a major driver. You can debate the exact numbers, but the direction is the important part: we are entering a period where the growth of AI is directly constrained by the ability of societies to build physical infrastructure quickly.

Reuters has reported that in the US, data centres could drive a large share of electricity demand growth through 2030, and that utility companies are receiving unprecedented power requests, with a meaningful fraction of planned projects facing delays due to grid constraints and infrastructure shortages. That is the collision: where software ambition meets the wall of physical lead times. I keep calling this a test because the outcome will teach us something about whether the Western system can still translate intelligence into physical throughput.

If the West cannot build enough power, enough data centres, and enough transmission to support its own AI ambitions, then it does not matter how good the models are. The models will be gated by the ability to pour concrete, approve projects, move electrons, build supply chains, and operate real systems. If, on the other hand, the AI era forces a return of operational seriousness, a return of prestige toward physical build, and a restored ability to execute large infrastructure projects, then it could partially counteract the trends that have been hollowing out the industrial layer. That is why I find the data centre buildout interesting in a way that most AI discourse misses. It is a litmus test of whether a society that has become extremely good at bits can still do atoms when atoms are what decide what is possible.

There is an obvious counterpoint people reach for when you talk about Western inability to build, and that counterpoint is Elon Musk, usually via Tesla and SpaceX. Musk could have done what many other extremely successful tech people did: stay in software, stay in finance, stay in platforms, stay in arenas where iteration is fast, and outcomes are legible in money metrics. Instead, he pushed into domains where the bottleneck is physical execution, manufacturing, supply chain, and operational risk.

Tesla and SpaceX are serious manufacturing and execution achievements, and they are also unusual in the modern American context, which is why they get cited so quickly. The fact that people can reach for one or two examples and everyone immediately knows what is being mentioned is, by itself, a signal. It suggests that the “industrialist who chooses the pain” pattern is not what the Western elite system normally produces.

Where this is going, and why it matters more in an AGI-shaped world

I think about AGI a lot, and I have tried to be careful about what I mean when I say it, because people use the term to mean everything from “chatbots with better reasoning” to “a system that can do nearly all cognitive work.” The dependence on physical capacity holds across that range, whatever timetable we attach to it.

Digital intelligence makes the digital world cheaper. Anything that can be done entirely in bits, or simulated entirely in bits, tends to accelerate as tools improve, because iteration costs fall and the ability to search solution spaces rises. That does not automatically mean the physical world accelerates at the same rate, because the physical world is gated by production, materials, energy, logistics, and coordination. If we get very strong systems for digital work, you can expect a lot of domains to move faster on the digital side: software production becomes more automated, digital design loops compress, research workflows accelerate, content production explodes, advertising and persuasion become more targeted and efficient, suggestion engines become sharper, financial strategy generation becomes more sophisticated. The “virtual” side of the economy, the part that can be copied and scaled at near-zero marginal cost, gets even more extreme.

That is a trap for societies that already over-reward the virtual side.

If the easiest way to deploy powerful digital intelligence is to make the attention machine more effective, make financial extraction more efficient, and make gambling interfaces more compelling, then you get a situation where the West takes the most effective tool it has ever built and pours it into the same sinks that have already been draining civic and industrial capacity. The better version is that strong AI turns the physical bottleneck into the main story, because the ability to deploy intelligence at scale becomes limited by power, compute, factories, robots, and supply chains. In that world, the societies that can build physical infrastructure quickly, reliably, and at scale have an advantage. This is why I do not think the after AGI story is automatically a story of instant physical abundance. The binding physical constraints endure for a long time, and those constraints are exactly what short-term, financialised societies struggle with.

As the models get smarter, I keep coming back to how serious a society is about building the physical layer through which that intelligence has to work.

The narrow reason for hope is that AI might force a partial rebalancing of incentives, simply because it is turning physical build into the choke point for the most valuable part of the software economy. If the frontier is compute, power, and data centre construction, then the people who can do operations, infrastructure, energy, robotics, manufacturing integration, and supply chain find themselves deciding whether the most valuable part of the software economy can keep progressing. That could pull talent back. It could pull prestige back. It could pull capital back. It could force the Western system to relearn some of the operational habits it has allowed to erode, because the market reward for physical throughput becomes too large to ignore.

The reason to be worried is that the system’s default reaction to new tools is to financialise them, platformise them, and turn them into extraction engines, because those paths fit the existing ladders and the existing short horizons. If we build a world where AI accelerates the attention machine, financial micro-optimisation, and gamblingification, then we get richer in the narrow sense and weaker in the fundamental sense. This is why I keep returning to the Chinese “real economy versus virtual economy” framing, even if I do not like the politics that often comes attached to it. The framing is right about one thing: a society that lets the virtual side dominate for long enough starts to forget how to do the real work. It starts to confuse money with power. It starts to confuse the capture of attention and the pricing of risk with progress itself. It starts to treat physical capacity as something you can always buy later, and then one day it discovers that you cannot buy later what you did not practice building for decades.

A society that only measures in dollars will eventually forget how to measure power.
