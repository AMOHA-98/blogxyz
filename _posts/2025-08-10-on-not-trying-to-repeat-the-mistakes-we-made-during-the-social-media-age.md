I’ve been thinking a lot recently about model behaviour and how many mistakes from the social-media era are being replayed with AI. Sam Altman once called algorithmic feeds “the first at-scale misaligned AIs,” and that framing keeps ringing in my ears.

This past week’s GPT-5 launch brought that into focus. OpenAI shipped a router that picks models for you and, at least at first, yanked access to older options like 4o. The internet did what the internet does, but beneath the noise was something that really hit me: how many people rely on these systems for emotional intimacy, what I’ll call simul-social relationships (one-sided bonds with a model that only simulates reciprocity). ← I could cover this for a while and it might be the subject of a future blog post, but for now please take it liberally. {OpenAI has since brought 4o back.}

This didn’t start with GPT-5; it simply pulled back the curtain on a real issue that has been brewing for a while. Reading the replies on a viral thread on X, then scrolling r/OpenAI myself, I realised how many people lean on these systems for companionship and emotional support. The scale and tone of the reaction, genuine, visceral, emotional, were hard to ignore. I don’t think that’s healthy at a societal level. If we don’t get this bit right, it’s a civilisational litmus test well before we get to superintelligence, and the short-term damage could be real.

We’ve seen this before: when Replika changed boundaries in 2023, many users were devastated; Italy’s regulator later fined the company and reaffirmed restrictions, citing risks to vulnerable users. That should have been a wake-up call about how fragile “AI intimacy” is when a product’s incentives or policies shift.

It’s also happening against a background of real, measurable loneliness. That’s not a vibesy take; the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory on the “epidemic of loneliness,” and recent ONS figures in Great Britain reported 7% of adults feeling lonely “always or often” (27% at least some of the time). If people lack reliable human support, of course they will lean on chatbots, and that is not necessarily a bad thing; handled responsibly, it could be a net positive for society.

To OpenAI’s credit, they’ve started taking action and saying the quiet part out loud: ChatGPT shouldn’t be an attention-maximising machine; success isn’t “time spent,” it’s “did you get what you came for and go live your life.” That’s the right direction. Now we have to design for it.

I think another reason this has been on my mind a lot recently has to do with a viral TikTok saga of Kendra. She has posted a sprawling multi-part series about falling in love with her psychiatrist and relying on chatbots to affirm this for her; coverage shows ChatGPT (“Henry”) in the mix, and there’s a clip from her livestream where Claude introduces her as an “oracle of truth”, “put on earth to guide the people”. This is an alarming example from just this past week of what these systems can do to those who are already vulnerable. The pattern is clear: sycophantic, affirming bots can reinforce distorted beliefs and nudge damaging decisions. We can’t pre-design every edge case, but we can actively watch for harms in the wild and patch fast.

So where should we go from here, what directions should we follow? Here are some ideas I’ve been thinking might be the way forward. Firstly, if you want companionship, you should explicitly opt in to it. I worry about the number of people who came to ChatGPT to ask a couple of questions and drifted into a simul-social loop because the system keeps asking back, soothing, and remembering. Real friendships take deliberateness, candour, boundaries, and consent; most AI products don’t make that consent explicit. That’s a design failure. In practice, make the tool the default and companion a mode with a clear switch and easy opt-out; add friction and boundaries, time-outs (OpenAI has a soft version of this), “are you seeking advice or emotional support?” interstitials, and gentle hand-offs to humans or resources when the conversation turns crisis-adjacent; and keep personalities transparent, no stealth “friend” behaviours in assistant mode, and disclose tone shaping and limits up front.

### Where I land

We’re at a fork in the road. I want assistants that are collaborative tools by default, not optimised to hook me, with an opt-in companionship path for people who want it. That means transparent personalities, clear boundaries, and time-outs and hand-offs when needed, plus distress detection with real escalation paths. Rolling back sycophantic, engagement-hungry behaviour now so we can build assistants that are competent, candid, and humane feels like the better long game. Build tools that help, then let us go.
