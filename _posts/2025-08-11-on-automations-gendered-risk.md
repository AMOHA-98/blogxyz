I have been circling this for a couple of years and not writing it down. Partly because it is touchy. Partly because I did not know how to talk about it publicly while hunting for jobs. Take this as a brain dump of ideas rather than truth. I want to provoke a useful argument and planning mindset, because I have not seen many people think this through.

The starting claim is simple. A lot of historic gains in women’s rights seem tightly tied to being needed at work. If you want to grow GDP, you cannot leave half the population on the bench. When women were required at scale, legal and civic inclusion often moved with that need. You can see it in the chronology. In the United Kingdom, wartime roles during 1914 to 1918 changed who did which jobs, then the Representation of the People Act 1918 gave some women the vote at 30 with property qualifications, and the Equal Franchise Act 1928 finally equalised the age threshold at 21. In France, women were enfranchised in 1944 while the country was mobilised for liberation and reconstruction. Elsewhere it did not always require war. New Zealand enfranchised women in 1893 after long petitioning. Finland moved to universal suffrage in 1906, including the right to stand for office. The point is not that economics explains everything. It is that demand for women’s labour has been one of the strongest engines pulling rights forward. 

Now flip the lens. When governments can fund themselves or grow output without mobilising broad labour, the link between “we need you at work” and “you gain rights and power” weakens. Oil is the obvious example from the last century. The rent financed the state, reduced pressure to tax and include, and in several cases correlated with lower female labour force participation and slower legal change. Saudi Arabia’s more recent shift is instructive in the other direction. Once the Vision 2030 diversification agenda made labour supply binding, high profile restrictions started to loosen. Women began driving in June 2018. Guardianship rules were eased in 2019. (None of this erases years of women’s activism in the Gulf, including the 1990 driving protests and the 2018 campaigns; the point is that in rent financed states the usual worker leverage is weaker because growth does not hinge on broad labour.) Female labour force participation moved into the mid 30s by 2024, beating the original 2030 target of 30 percent. None of that is linear or complete, but it is a clear example of policy following labour market arithmetic.

Here is the uncomfortable analogy. Treat automation as a synthetic resource. If AI and robots let you get more GDP from less human labour, you have effectively discovered domestic oil. That can be good for productivity and living standards. It can also weaken the disciplining force that historically pulled inclusion along with growth. And this is not a far future thing. The robot base is already large and still compounding. Annual industrial robot installations have topped half a million every year since 2021. The operational stock in factories reached around 4.28 million in 2023. Humanoid robots are now moving from demo to pilot in warehouses and on factory floors, aimed at the same task frontier as entry level labour. On the software side the big shops are saying that by 2030 roughly 27 to 30 percent of hours in the US and Europe could be automated in a midpoint adoption case, with generative models pulling timelines forward. Five to ten years is plausible. Fifteen would not shock me. The investment push is real already.

Where this bites is in who does which tasks. Generative models lean heavily on clerical, administrative, and some professional workflows. Those occupations are disproportionately female in many countries. In US data, executive secretaries and executive administrative assistants are about 95 percent women. The ILO’s cross country exposure work and the IMF’s 2024 Staff Discussion Note both put women at higher near-term exposure in high income economies, not because of innate traits but because of job mix. That does not mean job loss must be higher, but it does mean the transition risk is gendered if we do nothing. 
For the developing world the risk can run the other way. If cheap automation narrows the wage advantage in apparel, electronics assembly, and BPO, then some of the sectors that historically absorbed women at scale may shrink or move. Garment work in Asia is often majority female, frequently above 60 percent, and in some places nearer 80 percent. Voice based BPO also draws heavily on women, and several country studies now show high exposure to AI in exactly those service roles. The World Bank has been warning about this since at least 2017 in the manufacturing-led development context.

Demography overlays the whole picture. Total fertility is falling across most of the planet. The UN’s 2024 revision has global fertility around the low twos and already shows more than half of countries below replacement. The Lancet’s 2024 work projects that by mid-century the share of countries below replacement will be roughly three quarters. I am not trying to predict politics here, but it seems relevant that some wealthy democracies are already arguing about family size and pronatal policy, and the United States has seen a major change in abortion rights since 2022. I am noting this as a data point only. If fertility keeps dropping, some coalitions may respond by tightening the space around women rather than widening participation. This is a risk worth watching.

In the original draft for this blog I had an expansive section on care that I pulled back. Ageing societies need care, and parts of care will automate. Remote monitoring, scheduling, documentation, logistics, basic mobility devices. Men and women do care work, but in many countries women are overrepresented in paid care roles. If countries treat care as infrastructure and push up quality, it can be a dignified engine for work. If they do not, the sector risks staying low status and disproportionately absorbing women while higher status tasks automate away. Useful to keep in mind, not to lean on as the safety valve.

So, I am not arguing inevitability. I am arguing mechanism. When labour demand for women loosens, the historical rights accelerator weakens. When labour demand tightens, it strengthens. Automation can push either way depending on how we deploy it. If we let it act like a windfall, we will get windfall politics, fewer broad coalitions, fewer reasons to make inclusion pay, more tolerance for stalling rights where they are fragile. But I do not have a master plan. I do not know how to make labour “needed” again at scale, and I am not sure that would even hold in a world where capital deepens in software. The sharper way to state the worry is this: if growth decouples from people, rights decouple from growth. The next decade is the window where that decoupling either happens quietly or is made less likely. 

I think if we want to avoid a rights recession, we need to keep the growth and inclusion bargain alive in a world where the marginal worker is optional. I do not know the final answer. I am trying to name the risk clearly enough that better answers can compete with it.
