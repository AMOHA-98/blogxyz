This is probably draft thirty-something of this essay. I have written it as a UBI essay, an immigration essay, a capital versus labour essay, a housing rant, and every time it either blew up into a book or collapsed into a rant. So I parked it and went off to publish easier things instead.

What pulled me back was watching the discourse catch up. Over the last few months you can feel people arriving at adjacent versions of the same thought: cheap machine competence changes the distributional fight, and it changes it before we have built anything resembling a new settlement.

You should not read what follows as one neat argument delivered from top to bottom. It is closer to a set of linked essays, each one taking a swing at the same object from a different angle, each one trying to land its own point. The object is simple: intelligence on screens is getting cheap very fast, the physical world is staying slow and scarce, and politics has to absorb that mismatch.

I have been writing around this for a while. In the gendered automation piece I said automation starts to look like discovering a domestic resource, something like oil, where output stops being tightly tied to how many people you employ. In “Work After Work” I tried to describe what it felt like to walk into a labour market where the bottom of the white-collar ladder had already started to rot. I have written about the West’s strange mix of world-beating software and embarrassing physical capacity, about how happy it is to financialise everything and how allergic it is to building anything.

Underneath all of that is the same claim. The digital world is deflating, the physical world is not, and the gap between them is going to press on wages, welfare, borders, and the basic legitimacy of the deal, whether we like it or not.

I am not presenting this as a prophecy. Think of it as a model of what I have had in my head for the last couple of years, and what I am watching for over the next decade or two. Some of it will be wrong. That is fine. I care more about getting the shape of the pressure right than pretending I can time it.

I am going to keep using the phrase “digital deflation” for the thing at the centre of it. By that I mean the sliding cost of getting useful work out of machines on screens, as models eat white-collar tasks, in a world where rent, energy, childcare, food, trains, hospitals and concrete do not follow the same curve.

## digital deflation

If you have actually used the current models for anything serious, you do not need much convincing that something important shifted. Now frontier systems and the best open-source Chinese models sit in terminals and browsers and do real work. They draft and edit code, clean up writing, summarise long documents, translate, write briefs, run first-pass legal review, triage support tickets, poke at logs. You can chain a few together and suddenly the sort of work that used to require a team of juniors becomes something one person and a laptop can handle.

This is the part I was trying to get at in “Work After Work” before the models really hit. The rungs at the bottom of the white-collar ladder were already wobbling. Graduate schemes shrank, traineeships turned into unpaid experience, whole layers of boring but necessary office work got outsourced or automated away. Now managers have what they were missing then: a cheap, reasonably competent synthetic junior they can spin up on demand.

So the thing that is deflating here is not software, it is intelligence in the digital world. The kind of capability that used to require a human with a CV and a desk is increasingly bundled into the tool itself. Translation, summarising, drafting, basic analysis, routine support, all of that can be pushed through a model endpoint. The more of that you can do, and the more models sharpen, the less you have to pay for humans to sit in chairs and do it.

A lot of people still talk about these systems as “assistants”, as if they only add a thin layer of productivity and leave the shape of organisations intact. That is not how they will be used in the aggregate. Some firms will keep headcount flat and squeeze more output out of it; others will quietly stop hiring. If one human with a model can do what three humans did before, the going rate for that work moves, and it moves down, and a little bit of bargaining power leaks away every time.

This is what digital deflation looks like: a growing share of useful work that lives entirely on screens can be done by machines instead of by people, and even when you keep the people you can pair them with models and get far more out of them. The cost of doing a unit of digital work slides because you need fewer human hours for it and the marginal copy of the model is cheap to spin up. Every extra point of model quality reinforces that, and every open model that closes the gap pushes prices down further.

## bits, atoms, and why cheap intelligence does not make rent cheap

It is very tempting, especially if you live in the world of models and startups, to hear “things will get cheaper” and picture life getting cheaper. People can see the graphs: cost per token falls, task coverage rises, quality improves, and they imagine that curve spilling out into housing and food and transport. 

Some things will get cheaper. The parts people actually budget for will not, at least not automatically.

The digital world sits on a short clock. You write some code, you deploy it, you see what happens. If it is bad, you roll back. If it is good, you scale it. You can try twenty ideas in a week. The cost of failure is some annoyed users and an embarrassing changelog. Software runs on a compressed clock.

The physical world runs on a different clock entirely. Housing lives on ten to twenty year timelines. Power plants, substations and transmission lines do too. Rail projects can soak up half a working life. Hospitals and schools take years just to get through planning. Every decision involves planning rules, local politics, courts, environmental law, neighbours who do not want anything to change. You cannot roll back a tower block with a git revert. Housing, grids and rail live on multi-decade calendars.

For the last few decades, the way to “win” in the West was to avoid that clock. The US in particular let its industrial base run down, let its housing shortages compound, let its infrastructure age, while talent poured into software and finance. Europe did versions of this with more trains and a thicker safety net, but the same reflex was there: let somebody else do the hard manufacturing, we will do services and brains. China is the cleanest large-scale counterexample: it built ports, power, housing and factories first, then layered software on top.

Now, as cheap digital intelligence arrives, you get this weird crossed signal. In the realm of bits, the statement “things will get cheaper” is true. On-screen work is cheaper. Digital leisure is cheaper. Information is cheaper. The models will make it cheaper to do a large class of tasks that live entirely inside the glass, on screens.

None of that automatically applies to atoms.

Cheap intelligence does not magically make land cheap in London or San Francisco. It does not bulldoze zoning codes. It does not lay track or pour concrete or string high-voltage lines. It can help planners, it can help engineers, it can help with design and logistics and routing, but the binding constraint on most of the physical world is not “we could not think of a way to do this”, it is “we are not allowed, or we do not want, to do this”. When tech people talk about a deflationary future, they are usually mixing two very different things. The part that is true is all on the screen. The part that people hear includes their rent and their bills and their commute. That is the core trap. The deflation is real in the digital world, driven by automation of white-collar labour and the spread of models. The physical world, where most of your expenses actually sit, will only move if someone takes on the slow, ugly fights around building more of it.

So a country like the US can absolutely end up with near-agi models in every browser tab while keeping the same housing shortages, the same grid worries, the same broken transport. A country like China can plug similar models into a much bigger industrial base and is better positioned to turn cheap intelligence into cheap stuff.

## software brain tries to touch atoms

If you grow up in software, or in a culture built around it, you pick up a certain way of changing the world. You talk, you write, you code, you ship. You run experiments. You accept that you will be wrong a lot because you can roll back. You keep dashboards. You stare at graphs. You trust that if you move the right levers the system will respond in some intelligible way. That mindset is incredibly good at attacking problems that are already framed as information flows and incentives.

The trouble starts when people carry that mindset into the physical world and assume it still applies. You see it everywhere. In people who think a city can be “fixed” with an app and some data. You see it when people treat housing or healthcare or transport or education as if they are mainly execution problems with the right product team. You see it in the way some AI people talk about “using models to solve governance”, as if politics was mostly a shortage of good memos.

The world of atoms does not respond to pull requests. You do not patch a grid. You do not ship a new build of a railway and see if users prefer it. When you touch housing you touch land values, planning regimes, interests that have had decades to entrench themselves. When you touch energy you touch local pollution, national security, whole regions whose income depends on pipelines and wires. When you touch hospitals you touch unions, professional bodies, family expectations, fears about safety. You cannot A/B test your way around any of that.

The obvious steelman is that tech does build atoms. The hyperscalers have been doing large-scale hardware for years: datacentres, fibre, undersea cables, power contracts, sometimes whole substations. For a long time those projects were the physical world with a lot of the politics stripped out. They were privately financed, built on land you can buy, run behind a fence, optimised by one owner.

That may be changing. The next phase of AI is forcing these projects into the open: bigger footprints, bigger power draws, more visible tradeoffs, more local veto points. Datacentres are becoming a test of whether big tech can stomach the slow, contested world it usually talks around. If the politics gets hot enough, you can imagine the build moving elsewhere, not because the engineering is hard, but because permission is.

In the AI moment software brain shows up again in the way people talk about “AI for government” or “AI for climate” or “AI for cities”. The idea is that if you put enough intelligence on top of the system it will untangle itself. You get models that can draw up beautiful plans, optimise timetables, simulate policy changes, find fraud and waste, and the implicit hope is that this will finally let us treat the state like a large messy codebase that just needed better tooling. The catch is that the people most confident about a near-agi future are also the people least exposed to the physical bottlenecks. They live in the part of the economy where iteration is cheap and failure is reversible, so they expect the rest of the world to respond the same way.

What actually happens is much more boring. You get better slide decks. You get ministers reading summaries from models instead of summaries from staffers. You get another layer of advice on top of the same veto players. You still have to take decisions that anger voters and survive court challenges. You still have to persuade or batter your way past whatever interests profit from keeping the physical world scarce.

Models can still help in real ways. They can improve design, forecasting, scrutiny, logistics, procurement, scheduling. But intelligence is not the binding constraint. Capacity is. The hard part is whether you can close a lane of traffic and build a tram line without being removed from office. Whether you can authorise a new line of pylons across a county. Whether you can build dense housing near a station without being killed in the next election.

That is why you see some of the most enthusiastic talk about AI and deflation coming from people whose own lives have lived mostly inside the screen. They look at what models can do and assume the rest of life will track that curve. They underestimate how slow and stubborn the world of atoms is, and how much of that slowness is deliberate.

## automation as a resource and capital’s second shot

In a previous automation essay I said automation starts to look like discovering oil. Once you have good enough machines and good enough software, you get output that is not tightly tied to how many people you have sitting in factories or offices. Growth starts to come more from the stock of capital than from the flow of labour.

The interesting question is what happens once machines and models can supply most of the effort. If you can build robot fleets and datacentres that, combined, do a large share of what a modern economy needs, and if ownership of that capital is concentrated, then a world that did not quite exist in the past starts to come into view. Capital can push harder because it no longer has to bargain with a class of workers that can shut the country down. Each time a model replaces a human in the digital world, the credible threat of labour weakens a little. Each time a firm reorganises a process around machines instead of people, capital’s share of the outcome creeps up.

The long, slow failure to build enough housing and energy and transport at the same time makes it worse, because it means people have less room to move and more fear. You cannot easily walk away from a bad job in a city where you cannot find a flat and there is no other work.

I am not arguing that we are about to wake up in a pure robot-and-datacentre society. The point is that the direction is clear enough that it should change how we think about policy. For most of the industrial era there was a hard limit on how much you could push workers before they pushed back and it worked. In a world where more of the value creation happens on screens and in automated systems, that limit softens. This is the start of what I will later call human deflation: not only wages, but also leverage. Whether democracy and broad-based welfare survive stops being a question of mutual necessity and starts being a question of what the owners of the machines are prepared to tolerate.

## the middle gets cheap first

When people talk about AI and work they tend to jump to absolute statements. Either no one will have to work, or no one will have work. The reality is more specific. The first thing that gets cheap is entry-level and mid-level white-collar labour: careful, routine, cognitive work.

In “Work After Work” I tried to describe what that felt like even before this latest model wave hit. The grad jobs that used to be boring but reliable have already started to thin out. Trainee roles turned into unpaid internships. A lot of the sensible career paths already felt more like a queue with fewer doors at the end of it. Now models have arrived that can do huge chunks of what those grads were supposed to do. It is hard to look at that combination and not expect it to bite.

The people in the firing line are the ones whose job is to produce competent, checkable output all day. Writing usable copy, drafting emails, pulling data and making basic charts, reviewing simple contracts, handling predictable support tickets, turning tickets into patches, turning requirements into code. Paralegals and junior lawyers doing first-pass review. Analysts doing first-pass summaries. Junior developers doing the first draft of a feature and the tenth draft of glue code. You can see it in how these tools are deployed. They get used to raise throughput and to shrink the need for new hires at the margin. Teams use models for first-pass drafts, review, triage, code, investigation. The work still needs humans, but fewer of them, and more of their time shifts from doing the middle to supervising it, stitching outputs together, and catching errors. The cost of doing a unit of digital work drops because you need fewer human hours for it, and you can run as many copies of the model as you want at the same time.

This is what I meant earlier by human deflation: the slow slide in the price and bargaining power of human labour when models can take over more of what people do. Not just wages, but also leverage. Mid-curve office workers feel it first because their tasks line up neatly with what models are good at, but once they are easier to replace and easier to frighten, that shift bleeds outward. Everyone who used to rely on a stable, confident middle class ends up dealing with a poorer, more anxious version of it.

The trouble is that the people whose work is being undercut are also the ones who have to pay the thick end of the physical bill. They are the ones renting, trying to have kids, paying for childcare, riding broken transport, living inside the slow clock of atoms. Their expenses are on the physical side where nothing is trending to zero. Their bargaining power sits on the digital side where the price of what they do for a living is sliding down, and with it some of the political leverage that used to come from being hard to replace.

One useful way to frame this is that capability will outpace our ability to reorganise society, and the first group to get punched in the face by that gap is the middle of the bell curve. The very normal, very average office workers who were supposed to be the backbone of the middle class find themselves competing with a machine, paying for a life that is still priced as if they had all the leverage.

## ageing, automation, migration, remittances

Now put digital deflation into an ageing society and ask what happens to migration. For a long time, rich countries could tell themselves a simple story: you need a steady inflow of young workers to keep the dependency ratio from snapping, to staff services, to pay into pensions, to keep the machine running. Cheap automation weakens that claim. If machines and models can cover more of the work inside your own borders, “we need migrants” starts to sound less like a structural necessity and more like a choice.

This is the border version of the same story: digital deflation changes the price of on-screen work, and human deflation changes the bargaining position of labour, and migration has always been one of labour’s escape valves.

In elite interviews you can hear the new confidence: once AI is good enough, it becomes harder to justify large-scale immigration except for very specialised skills. I’m not really interested in making a moral argument here. I’m pointing at a shift in what feels thinkable: in an automated, digital-heavy economy, rich countries can imagine running with fewer imported people.

They will still need some care workers, some builders, some very skilled engineers and scientists, and some roles remain stubbornly physical. And some of this plays out on a longer clock: robots and physical automation take longer to diffuse than models on screens. The timing will vary by sector and country, and some of the downstream effects here may take longer than the current argument cycle wants to allow. But the old framing of migration as a fiscal lifeline weakens when the on-screen part of the economy is deflating and the marginal worker is easier to substitute. The same on-screen price slide that undercuts mid-level white-collar labour also gives states and firms more confidence that they can “make do” with fewer new humans on their own soil.

A lot of countries have built part of their growth model around exporting humans. India is the standout example. Millions leave, work abroad, send money back, and support whole families and local economies. Parts of Africa and Asia follow a similar pattern. Remittances are a pillar: they pay rent, school fees, food, and debt, and whole local economies end up leaning on them.

Outsourcing already moved jobs to cheaper places. Covid just normalised doing more of that work from anywhere, and made it easier to import labour without importing people. Firms could hire abroad without relocation packages, without visas, without the politics of migration. Call centres in the Philippines and India, back offices in Bangladesh, routine ops done from a different continent. You could earn far more than the local norm by sitting on the far end of a fibre line and doing work for a rich-country firm.

Now imagine both doors tightening at once. If the US, UK, Canada, Germany, the Nordics decide they only want a small, hyper-selected flow of migrants, and if AI plus robots can cover more of what migrants used to do, the export-people-for-remittance model hits a wall. At the same time, models start to chew through the remote work that rode on top of outsourcing. The call centre agent who replaced an underpaid junior in London finds herself competing with a bot that never sleeps. The offshore ops worker who used to watch a handful of feeds finds himself competing with a system that can watch twenty at once for less than his wage.

On the receiving side, politics has already shifted in that direction. The Nordics are the standard example: generous welfare states, increasingly comfortable linking benefits and borders, treating the welfare system as something built for “us” and defended by tight control over “them”. The broader West has become more anti-immigrant over the last decade while its voters also compete more aggressively over scarce housing, school places and services. So you end up with rich, ageing societies that feel less economic pressure to open the door, more political pressure to keep it shut, and more capacity to automate both the jobs migrants did in person and the jobs they were hoping to do remotely. On the other side you have sending countries where millions of young people were raised with the expectation that they would leave and send money back, or sit in a call centre plugged into a foreign firm, now facing a narrower set of destinations, fewer remote ladders, and a home state that has not built enough capacity to absorb them.

## cash, pensions, and the frozen future

The existing welfare structure in a lot of rich countries does not help with any of this, because it is pointed at the past. Formally it looks like a set of neutral programmes: pensions, healthcare, unemployment, disability, tax credits. In practice the biggest cheques and the strongest guarantees skew toward older people, because that is where the system’s promises sit. On top of that, across a lot of the rich world, policy has spent decades quietly tying stability to house prices and retirement portfolios. If you bought at the right time, the state and central bank became your partner. If you were born later and came of age in a tighter, more expensive world, your job was to pay in and wait.

Covid made one thing obvious: when a crisis hits, rich states can move money fast. They can write cheques, expand benefits, run furlough schemes, backstop balance sheets. What they cannot do quickly is add real capacity: trained staff, hospital throughput, childcare slots, grid headroom. Cash runs on the short clock. The physical world does not.

Once you add digital deflation, cheap intelligence helps firms and asset owners first: they can substitute machines for people, widen margins, and capture more of the upside. More workers, meanwhile, feel their wages and leverage soften, and political demand shifts toward cushioning. The fiscal space for that cushioning is already heavily committed to old promises, so the reflex is to layer more cash on top. Whether that cash is funded by borrowing or by taxing the rich is not the main point. The main point is what happens when you pour money into a system that cannot expand the scarce stuff people actually need.

This is why I keep coming back to cash as a trap. In a world where work gets cheaper but the physical bottlenecks stay tight, more transfers mostly bid up the price of whatever is scarce. Landlords capture housing support. Private providers capture healthcare subsidies. Energy firms capture bill help. People feel marginally better for a moment, then watch the cost of living climb again, and the cheque turns back into a gesture.

Younger workers watch a system that will spend heavily to preserve pension promises and asset prices, then offer them stipends and one-off payments as a response to automation. Older voters who feel their own position is precarious see any new cash scheme as a threat to what they have. The stage is set for a fight over fixed pots rather than a serious attempt to change the shape of the material world.

None of this says “never do cash”. In a crisis, or as a buffer, it matters. The point is that transfers are the easiest lever to pull, and states that lean on easy levers while ignoring hard ones will keep doing that. In an AI world that habit gets more dangerous, not less, because it turns the politics into a loop: wages soften, transfers rise, bottlenecks bite, prices climb, and the answer becomes another transfer.

The obvious objection is “fine, then tax the winners and redistribute harder”. But redistribution that stays in cash still runs into the same wall: if the scarce parts of life cannot expand, the money just gets converted into higher rents, higher bills, and higher fees. If you want transfers to actually stick, you have to change what they are landing on. You need welfare that lives in the physical world.

## welfare as concrete

There is at least one other instinct on offer, and it starts from the premise that welfare has to live in the physical world. You can see it most clearly in build-first states: the idea that the first job is not to wire an income floor into every account, but to expand the stock of housing, energy, transport, and basic capacity so life stops feeling like a constant auction. In the language around “common prosperity”, Chinese leaders warn against slipping into a “welfarism” trap: a population that expects large cash transfers, a culture of dependency, the social rot that follows. The instinct that follows is not “wire an income floor into every account”. It is build-out. Housing, rail, ports, power, factories. Capacity as the first instrument, cash as the secondary one.

The result, in build-first systems, is a model where welfare is stored in concrete, steel and throughput, unevenly and not always fairly. Chinese citizens do not get a universal income floor wired into their accounts; they get a country that built a lot of physical stuff in a short time and a promise that more of that will arrive. They live in and on top of their welfare. The Western citizen gets a more complicated bundle of rights and payments and a lot of talk about the magic of markets, then discovers that a huge share of that bundle is locked inside someone else’s house and someone else’s pension fund.

In a world where intelligence on screens is cheap, welfare as concrete has one clear advantage. It gives you somewhere to plug that intelligence that is not just another app or internal tool. If you already have the capacity to build, cheap models help you design better factories, run better logistics, schedule trains and power, plan cities, run hospitals. The marginal gain from adding brains to a working physical system is huge. If your physical base is weak, the same models will write beautiful plans that sit in drawers.

None of this is an argument that China has solved welfare, or that its political system is a template. It has its own pathologies: consumer weakness, uneven provision, and a lot of the downside of a build-first model. The point is narrower. The instinct about where welfare should live in the first place is closer to what an AI century demands than ours.

If we carry our own instinct into a period of rapid digital deflation, we will keep reaching for the wrong tool. We will talk about laying a basic income on top of a housing crisis instead of attacking the housing crisis. We will talk about AI upskilling instead of building enough real world throughput. We will let the winners of the model phase feed off the old physical bottlenecks and then drip some of that back as philanthropy or schemes.

A better answer is a middle ground: treat concrete as the core of welfare, and treat cash as the last layer. Enough housing that rent is boring. Enough grid that power is cheap. Enough transport that the geography of your city does not decide your life. Enough clinics and schools that waiting lists are not a constant background noise. Then, on top of that, you add money and models.

If you do that, cheap intelligence is genuinely liberating. If you do not, it just makes the contrast starker between what people see on their screens and the life they can actually afford, and human deflation bites much harder than it needed to.

## welfare chauvinism and the new nativism

For a long time the anti-immigration story and the pro-immigration story argued past each other. One side said there is a fixed pot: more people means thinner slices, protect the pot. The other said we can grow the pie: more people means more workers, more taxpayers, more dynamism, everyone wins. That pro-immigration line is only persuasive if capacity expands with population, if housing, energy, schools, clinics, transport, childcare can scale without turning politics into trench warfare. In a world where digital work gets cheaper because models do it, and the physical world stays scarce because nobody builds, the fixed-pot people start to look less like paranoids and more like realists.

You can already see the shape of it in the places that actually run high-trust welfare states. The Nordics are the cleanest example. They built generous systems when they were smaller and more homogeneous; over time many of them moved toward tighter immigration and tighter rules around access to benefits. Inside that frame the logic is not hard to understand: citizens paid in, citizens get it, and if you open the circle too widely you risk breaking the trust that keeps the whole thing funded. Add digital deflation on top and the politics sharpens. Once a larger share of your own middle class is pushed onto some mix of subsidies, tax credits, and state support, they stop seeing welfare as something for “other people” and start seeing it as their own floor. When their wages are under pressure from models and their costs are high because the physical world is constrained, they become hypersensitive to anything that looks like competition for scarce support. Human deflation turns them from comfortable contributors into anxious claimants, watching the door.

I am not making a moral argument here about who “deserves” what, or pretending that migrants are automatically entitled to a state’s resources. I am pointing at a shift in what becomes politically stable when scarcity is real and labour is easier to substitute.

It is also not obvious that this is simply bad. A state is allowed to ration membership. If you are running a high-benefit system under real scarcity, a tighter definition of “us” can be a coherent way to keep it funded, and a coherent way to keep politics from tearing itself apart. The deeper claim here is that we may be moving into a world with less churn: fewer people relocating across borders, fewer countries treating migration as an economic release valve, something closer to a post-peak-globalism equilibrium.

None of this starts from a blank slate: rich countries have been selecting for high-skill migrants for years, using points systems, thresholds, sponsorship and narrow routes that filter who gets in and on what terms. The shift is the next step: moving from “we select” to “we might not need much volume at all.”

The pro-immigration “we can grow the pie” line depends on doing the hard part: building. More homes, more grid, more transport, more clinics, more schools, more childcare. In much of Europe and North America that is technically feasible; the land and capital exist, the constraints are political and institutional. Instead many places chose something closer to artificial scarcity: freeze housing through planning, drag infrastructure through permitting, let services run hot, then act surprised when voters behave as if they live on a small island with finite rations.

In that world, restrictionist politics gets new fuel, and it stops being confined to the old right. The centre of gravity shifts to people who live closest to the bottlenecks: renters, squeezed middle-class families, anyone competing for housing, childcare, school places, clinics, and a shrinking sense of state capacity. It is a middle class watching the white-collar ladder get burned from both ends: fewer entry routes, fewer protected roles, and a growing sense that the state’s promises are already fully mortgaged. Digital deflation eats the job base, human deflation eats the leverage, and the politics shifts from “how do we grow” to “who exactly is this for”. The rhetorical packaging will often be softer than the instinct: “reform”, “fairness”, “integration”, “prioritising citizens”, sometimes “repatriation”. The underlying move is the same: draw a harder line around who counts as a claimant, because the circle starts to feel like the last remaining asset.

If we are not careful, the new settlement is a very particular creature: a UBI-flavoured welfare state funded by automated capital, paired with a very hard boundary.

## everything but money

If you put all of this together, the version of welfare people keep reaching for, including UBI, only works as a last layer, not as the foundation. Cash is fast, politically legible, and easy to scale, which is exactly why it becomes the default response, but in a world where the on-screen economy is deflating and the physical economy is bottlenecked, cash-first policy mostly just routes more demand into the same scarce pipes.

What matters first is not the slogan, it is the throughput. Housing that can expand without every marginal unit becoming a political crisis, energy that is cheap and stable enough to power both ordinary life and datacentres without turning winters into panic, and basic public capacity in transport and healthcare that makes disruption survivable rather than existential. None of that is about whether we can think of better plans. We can. Models will help with design, logistics, scheduling, forecasting, procurement, oversight. They will be force multipliers for anyone who is already allowed to build. But they do not dissolve veto points, they do not shorten planning calendars by themselves, and they do not make land, power, clinics, staff, or track appear on command.

If you actually manage to do that build-out, then cash becomes what it is supposed to be: insurance and slack. In a world where rent is boring, energy is predictable, transport works, and waiting lists are not a constant background threat, a basic income or a tax credit can smooth the swings of an AI-heavy economy instead of becoming a subsidy to landlords, private providers, and every other owner of scarcity. The order matters. Concrete first, cash last.

My fear is that the West will not do the order correctly. The political system is too gummed up, too vetoed, too dependent on asset prices, too willing to treat redistribution as a substitute for construction. We will tax the rich, we will tax the upper middle, we will spray transfers and credits and rebates through a system that cannot expand the things people actually need, and the white-collar middle that is getting undercut by models will become a hornets nest: more anxious, more politicised, more willing to fight over a fixed pot, because the physical world underneath them is still tight.

So we still get digital deflation. Intelligence on screens keeps getting cheaper, capital keeps finding ways to own more of it, and the middle of the labour market keeps getting chewed up. The only question is what that looks like in lived reality. In one branch, cheap intelligence lands on top of a society that has rebuilt enough capacity that people can absorb the shock and re-route their lives without falling apart. In the other, we get something closer to a software-only singularity: near-agi in every workflow, and the same expensive, scarce, permissioned physical world underneath it, where life does not get much easier even as the screen gets godlike.

Intelligence is becoming abundant. Agency is not. The defining question of this century is whether we can turn cheap cognition into real capacity, or whether it stays trapped on screens while scarcity hardens everywhere else.
