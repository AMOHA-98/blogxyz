This is probably draft thirty-something of this essay. I have written it as a UBI essay, an immigration essay, a capital versus labour essay, a housing rant, and every time it either blew up into a book or collapsed into a rant. So I parked it and went off to publish easier things instead.

What pulled me back was watching the discourse catch up. Over the last few months you can feel people arriving at adjacent versions of the same thought: cheap machine competence changes the distributional fight, and it changes it before we have built anything resembling a new settlement around it.

What follows is closer to a set of linked swings at the same object than one neat argument from top to bottom. The object is simple: intelligence on screens is getting cheap very fast, the physical world is staying slow and scarce, and politics has to absorb that mismatch.

I have been writing around this for a while. In the gendered automation piece I said automation starts to look like discovering a domestic resource, something like oil, where output stops being tightly tied to how many people you employ. In â€œWork After Workâ€ I tried to describe what it felt like to walk into a labour market where the bottom of the white-collar ladder had already started to rot. I have written about the Westâ€™s odd mix of world-beating software and embarrassing physical capacity, about how happy it is to financialise everything, and how allergic it is to building anything.

Underneath all of that is the same claim. The digital world is deflating, the physical world is not, and the gap between them is going to press on wages, welfare, borders, and the basic legitimacy of the deal, whether we like it or not.

Think of this as a model of what I have had in my head for the last couple of years and what I am watching for over the next decade or two, not a prophecy. Some of it will be wrong, and that is fine. I care more about getting the shape of the pressure right than pretending I can time it.

I am going to keep using the phrase â€œdigital deflationâ€ for the thing at the centre of it. By that I mean the sliding cost of getting useful work out of machines on screens, as models eat white-collar tasks, in a world where rent, energy, childcare, food, trains, hospitals and concrete do not follow the same curve.

## digital deflation

If you have actually used the current models for anything serious, you do not need much convincing that something important shifted. Now frontier systems and the best open-source Chinese models sit in terminals and browsers and do real work. They draft and edit code, clean up writing, summarise long documents, translate, write briefs, run first-pass legal review, triage support tickets, poke at logs. You can chain a few together and suddenly the sort of work that used to require a team of juniors becomes something one person and a laptop can handle.

This is the part I was trying to get at in â€œWork After Workâ€ before the models really hit. The rungs at the bottom of the white-collar ladder were already wobbling. Graduate schemes shrank, traineeships turned into unpaid experience, whole layers of boring but necessary office work got outsourced or automated away. Now managers have what they were missing then: a cheap, reasonably competent synthetic junior they can spin up on demand.

So the thing deflating here is intelligence in the digital world. The kind of capability that used to require a human with a CV and a desk is increasingly bundled into the tool itself. Translation, summarising, drafting, basic analysis, routine support, all of that can be pushed through a model endpoint. The more of that you can do, and the more models sharpen, the less you have to pay for humans to sit in chairs and do it.

A lot of people still talk about these systems as assistants, as if they only add a thin layer of productivity and leave the rest of organisations intact. In the aggregate they will be used more harshly than that. Some firms will keep headcount flat and squeeze more output out of it; others will quietly stop hiring. If one human with a model can do what three humans did before, the going rate for that work moves, and it moves down, and a little bit of bargaining power leaks away every time.

This is what digital deflation looks like a growing share of useful work that lives entirely on screens can be done by machines instead of by people, and even when you keep the people you can pair them with models and get far more out of them. The cost of doing a unit of digital work slides because you need fewer human hours for it and the marginal copy of the model is cheap to spin up. Every extra point of model quality reinforces that, and every open model that closes the gap pushes prices down further.

## bits, atoms, and why cheap intelligence does not make rent cheap

It is very tempting, especially if you live in the world of models and startups, to hear â€œthings will get cheaperâ€ and picture life getting cheaper. People can see the graphs: cost per token falls, task coverage rises, quality improves, and they imagine that curve spilling out into housing and food and transport. 

Some things will get cheaper. The parts people actually budget for will not, at least not automatically.

The digital world sits on a short clock. You write some code, you deploy it, you see what happens. If it is bad, you roll back. If it is good, you scale it. You can try twenty ideas in a week. The cost of failure is some annoyed users and an embarrassing changelog. Software runs on a compressed clock.

The physical world runs on a different clock entirely. Housing lives on ten to twenty year timelines. Power plants, substations and transmission lines do too. Rail projects can soak up half a working life. Hospitals and schools take years just to get through planning. Every resolution involves planning rules, local politics, courts, environmental law, neighbours who do not want anything to change. You cannot roll back a tower block with a git revert. Housing, grids and rail live on multi-decade calendars.

For the last few decades, the way to win in the West was to avoid that clock. The US in particular let its industrial base run down, let its housing shortages compound, let its infrastructure age, while talent poured into software and finance. Europe did versions of this with more trains and a thicker safety net, but the same reflex was there: let somebody else do the hard manufacturing, we will do services and brains. China is the cleanest large-scale counterexample: it built ports, power, housing and factories first, then layered software on top.

Now, as cheap digital intelligence arrives, you get this weird crossed signal. In the space of bits, the statement â€œthings will get cheaperâ€ is true. On-screen work is cheaper. Digital leisure is cheaper. Information is cheaper. The models will make it cheaper to do a large class of tasks that live entirely inside screens.

None of that automatically applies to atoms.

Cheap intelligence does not magically make land cheap in London or San Francisco. It does not bulldoze zoning codes. It does not lay track or pour concrete or string high-voltage lines. It can help planners, it can help engineers, it can help with design and logistics and routing, but the hard constraint on most of the physical world is permission. We know how to build all of it. We are not allowed to, or we do not want to. When tech people talk about a deflationary future, they are usually mixing two very different things. The part that is true is all on the screen. The part that people hear includes their rent and their bills and their commute. That is the trap I keep worrying about. The deflation is real in the digital world, driven by automation of white-collar labour and the spread of models. The physical world, where most of your expenses actually sit, will only move if someone takes on the slow, ugly fights around building more of it.

So a country like the US can absolutely end up with near-agi models in every browser tab while keeping the same housing shortages, the same grid worries, the same broken transport. A country like China can plug similar models into a much bigger industrial base and is well positioned to turn cheap intelligence into cheap stuff.

## software brain tries to touch atoms

If you grow up in software, or in a culture built around it, you pick up a certain way of changing the world. You talk, you write, you code, you ship. You run experiments. You accept that you will be wrong a lot because you can roll back. You keep dashboards. You stare at graphs. You trust that if you move the right levers the system will respond in some intelligible way. That mindset is incredibly good at attacking problems that are already framed as information flows and incentives.

The trouble starts when people carry that mindset into the physical world and assume it still applies. You see it everywhere. In people who think a city can be â€œfixedâ€ with an app and some data. You see it when people treat housing or healthcare or transport or education as if they are mainly execution problems with the right product team. You see it in the way some AI people talk about using models to solve governance, as if politics was mostly a shortage of good memos.

The world of atoms does not respond to pull requests. You do not patch a grid. You do not ship a new build of a railway and see if users prefer it. When you touch housing you touch land values, planning regimes, interests that have had decades to entrench themselves. When you touch energy you touch local pollution, national security, whole regions whose income depends on pipelines and wires. When you touch hospitals you touch unions, professional bodies, family expectations, fears about safety. You cannot A/B test your way around any of that.

The fair pushback here is that tech does build atoms. The hyperscalers have been doing large-scale hardware for years: datacentres, fibre, undersea cables, power contracts, sometimes whole substations. For a long time those projects were the physical world with a lot of the politics stripped out. They were privately financed, built on land you can buy, run behind a fence, optimised by one owner.

That may be changing. The next phase of AI is forcing these projects into the open: bigger footprints, bigger power draws, more visible tradeoffs, more local veto points. Datacentres are becoming a test of whether big tech can stomach the slow, contested world it usually talks around. If the politics gets hot enough, you can imagine the build moving elsewhere, because permission is harder than the engineering.

In the AI moment software brain shows up again in the way people talk about â€œAI for governmentâ€ or â€œAI for climateâ€ or â€œAI for citiesâ€. The idea is that if you put enough intelligence on top of the system it will untangle itself. You get models that can draw up beautiful plans, optimise timetables, simulate policy changes, find fraud and waste, and the implicit hope is that this will finally let us treat the state like a large messy codebase that just needed better tooling. The people most confident about a near-agi future are also the people least exposed to the physical bottlenecks. They live in the part of the economy where iteration is cheap and failure is reversible, so they expect the rest of the world to respond the same way.

What actually happens is much more boring. You get better slide decks. You get ministers reading summaries from models instead of summaries from staffers. You get another layer of advice on top of the same veto players. You still have to take decisions that anger voters and survive court challenges. You still have to persuade or batter your way past whatever interests profit from keeping the physical world scarce.

Models can still help in real ways. They can improve design, forecasting, scrutiny, logistics, procurement, scheduling. But intelligence is not the hard part. Capacity is. The hard part is whether you can close a lane of traffic and build a tram line without being removed from office. Whether you can authorise a new line of pylons across a county. Whether you can build dense housing near a station without being killed in the next election.

I think that is why you see some of the most enthusiastic talk about AI and deflation coming from people whose own lives have lived mostly inside the screen. They look at what models can do and assume the rest of life will track that curve. They underestimate how slow and stubborn the world of atoms is, and how much of that slowness is deliberate.

## automation as a resource and capitalâ€™s second shot

In a previous automation essay I said automation starts to look like discovering oil. Once you have good enough machines and good enough software, you get output that is not tightly tied to how many people you have sitting in factories or offices. Growth starts to come more from the stock of capital than from the flow of labour.

The interesting question is what happens once machines and models can supply most of the effort. If you can build robot fleets and datacentres that, combined, do a large share of what a modern economy needs, and if ownership of that capital is concentrated, then a world that did not quite exist in the past starts to come into view. Capital can push harder because it no longer has to bargain with a class of workers that can shut the country down. Each time a model replaces a human in the digital world, the credible threat of labour weakens a little. Each time a firm reorganises a process around machines instead of people, capitalâ€™s share of the outcome creeps up.

The long, slow failure to build enough housing and energy and transport at the same time makes it worse, because it means people have less room to move and more fear. You cannot easily walk away from a bad job in a city where you cannot find a flat and there is no other work.

We are not about to wake up in a pure robot-and-datacentre society, but the direction is clear enough that it should change how we think about policy. For most of the industrial era there was a hard limit on how much you could push workers before they pushed back and it worked. In a world where more of the value creation happens on screens and in automated systems, that limit softens. This is the start of what I later call human deflation: not only wages, but political leverage too. Whether democracy and broad-based welfare survive becomes a question of what the owners of the machines are prepared to tolerate.
