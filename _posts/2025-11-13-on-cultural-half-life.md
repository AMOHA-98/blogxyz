A while ago I was swapping screenshots with a designer friend. We were both poking at the new generation of model based tools. His complaint was simple: “Everything looks like a SaaS landing page from about five years ago.” Purple gradients, friendly avatars, soft cards, the usual pattern from old dribbble boards. If you gave the system very careful art direction, you could drag it out of that hole, but the default kept snapping back to the same safe starter kit.

On the writing side I was seeing the same thing. Long passages that are perfectly serviceable on the surface and dead in the middle. You get a steady tempo of topic sentences plus polite hedging. You get metaphors that were on slide decks at FAANG companies in 2016. You get the feeling that you are reading a composite of every high performing Medium post that has ever existed, sliced into paragraphs and reassembled in front of you.

If you work with these systems for a while you start to feel where that comes from: pretraining takes in an absurd pile of data, gradient descent nudges the weights until the model gets better at guessing the next token, the next pixel pattern, and the easiest way to do well on that task is to sit close to the average, leaning toward phrases and layouts that appear often and rarely get punished. Then post training shows up with human feedback and safety limitations and “be helpful, be harmless, do not be weird”, and it squeezes even more of the texture out.

What you end up talking to is something like a centroid of our public output, polished by a bunch of corrections.

On the design side the same story plays out because the web has been filling up with tasteful SaaS layout kits for more than a decade, all of them borrowing from one another: card decks and three-column feature sections and mid tone gradients and the same sort of illustration where everyone is smiling and slightly off balance. Feed that into a model and you are giving it a strong signal about what “looks right” when someone says “modern product site”, while the model training loop has no reason to prefer the rare layout that only appears on one weird studio’s portfolio.

We were already living in a kind of cultural remix loop before any of this. Old styles keep coming back with a clever wink. Films reference older films. Songs borrow hooks from the back catalogue. Mark Fisher called that atmosphere hauntological, a sense that culture is passing through its own archive rather than walking toward anything new. A large model feels like that mood turned into infrastructure. It digests the public record and turns it into a thing you can query, so you end up talking to a compressed memory of what we have already done.

If you spend a lot of time around writers and designers, that begins to grate. You know how much of their taste comes from things you cannot scrape: the arguments in group chats, the years spent caring about line height, the failed drafts nobody ever posted. You also know that most users will not notice the difference, at least at first. A lot of people will look at the model-made versions and shrug. It works, ship it.

That gap in reaction is where this idea of a cultural half life started for me, because I kept thinking about how quickly an aesthetic now moves from someone’s thing to generic slop, how social media had already done some of this, and how generative models just press the pedal down.

Before any of that, taste was tighter to actual people. You wanted a particular designer because you had seen their work and felt drawn to it. You liked how they handled type on a specific poster. You liked the way they made small, slight decisions in their layouts. You knew they had gone through a path that gave them those preferences: art school, certain jobs, the years they spent obsessed with some obscure studio in a different country.

The same principle holds for writing: trust usually attaches to a name. At some point you read an essay that made you stop scrolling and now you follow that person around. It is about the pattern in how they see the world, and you choose to sign up for that pattern. They are one person, with one set of scars and jokes and blind spots. They can only write so much. If they spend a month working on something for you, that is a month they are not spending on something else.

Taste in that world means you are in contact with a limited mind. There is scarcity baked into it. Scarcity of focus, scarcity of hours, scarcity of whatever strange path produced that sensibility.

Then the feed arrived and started to play with the plumbing. Suddenly scenes that would once have stayed local showed up in your pocket. You no longer had to live in a city with a gallery to see certain artists. You did not have to know the right people in nightlife to find a micro trend. People mined niche aesthetics and spun them up into content on schedule. Content operations became a kind of factory job: pull from the archive, remix into a short clip, schedule, repeat. Faces and places changed, the pipeline stayed the same.

This made aesthetics travel faster, but it also introduced a different sort of gate: algorithms decided whose work landed in your feed, and if the system decided you were into a certain look, it poured more of it on you. Whole styles could exist next to one another without ever crossing feeds, and people lived inside these invisible partitions and thought they were seeing the whole surface.

Even then there was friction. You could watch a video of a party and still not get through the door. You could admire a wardrobe and still not have the money for it. You could scroll through interior design accounts while sitting in a room that did not look like any of them. The gap between seeing an aesthetic and inhabiting it was still thick with practical limits.

Generative models eat some of that gap, especially the part where taste is turned into media, because you no longer have to spend months learning a layout tool before you put together something that looks believable, and you do not have to practice copywriting for years before you ship text that sounds “professional”. You ask a system to do it and adjust the prompts until the output passes a basic sniff test.

Under the hood it is still gradient descent on a giant dataset, plus a layer of human fine tuning: pretraining pulls the model toward the thickest clusters in the data, post training shaves away edges that offended raters, and what survives is a feel and an aesthetic sense that feels acceptable to the broadest slice of people involved in the training process. That is the point really, a model that regularly produced deeply strange or unpleasant results would not last long in a product.

When that sort of model was locked to a research lab, it was an oddity. Now it runs inside search, office suites, slide tools, design tools, developer tools. It starts to function as a general-purpose taste server, something people reach for in the course of ordinary work. A common mind that you can ask for diagrams, taglines, thumbnails, layouts, scripts. It will answer in the voice it has been sculpted into: helpful, steady, slightly bland.

If taste once meant “this one person, with this particular life, chose these details for you”, the new arrangement leans toward “this shared model, built from a huge pile of past work, produced something that looks close enough.” The first version comes with scarcity baked in and the second does not, so when everyone can sit on top of the same synthetic author, taste shifts from being a proof that you knew a mind into something closer to the house style of a platform.

Half life drops when a new visual trick or essay cadence moves from someone’s signature into the training pool; it lands, spreads, starts to live in the data, and then the model can reproduce it. Once that happens, every marketing team under time pressure can lean on it as a preset, and whoever runs content ops in those teams can get by with decent prompts and a sense of what will pass procurement, without ever hiring the people who developed the style.

An aesthetic that once took years to diffuse can now be boiled down and copied in days. You can rent a compressed imitation from a model API without going through the apprenticeship, joining the scene that birthed it, or reading the same books. The look becomes available to you while the sensibility behind it remains something you have barely encountered.

At that point the style starts to die through sheer familiarity. It can still be good work and already feel like background. Open a dozen SaaS sites and they all have the same balance of colour and copy. Open ten AI-written blog posts, and they all feel like they are arguing in the same register. Only the author and a small group of obsessives remember that it ever felt new.

There is a phrase I keep using in my notes for our current culture, “aesthetic grazing”, people moving through feeds at speed, snacking on micro trends without ever stopping long enough to digest any of them properly. The combination of short-form video, push notifications, and low-effort production makes this a natural way to live online, and generative models feed that existing tendency by turning the supply up to a setting the older content factories couldn't reach.

Amid all this, people will still do careful work; they always will, but the surrounding temperature changes. If the baseline of acceptable writing and design is provided by a synthetic mind trained on giant corpora, then human work that sits near that baseline will merge into it, and the only things that stand out will be either much worse or much stranger.

A piece of strange horror fiction on Reddit called Mother Horse Eyes has stuck with me for years (worth reading if you have time). Scattered through it is this idea of people called mixers. Their job is to sit in front of a stream of media and stitch together new combinations for an audience that has long since burned through ordinary entertainment. Old films, ads, fragments of novels, all thrown into a pot until something hits. The people consuming what they make have spent so long inside this system that nothing holds their attention for more than a moment.

When I look at the way models sit underneath content tools, I keep thinking about those mixers, because a version of that role is already appearing: people who specialise in prompting, who know how to steer image models into current fashion, who know how to get copy that passes brand review. Their skill is in operating the machine that sculpts a flood of work. The unit is the pipeline.

Layer that pipeline on top of a culture already full of aesthetic grazers and you get a very specific kind of churn, where any look or style that lands is almost immediately hauled into a content schedule. Variations get tested, the better ones get boosted, and the model shifts its sense of “good” a little further toward them; feed yet another generation of that work back in, and the curve gets steeper.

If you care about taste in any serious way, this all feels a little cursed. I keep finding that the old signals no longer tell me what I used to read into them. Owning a rare piece of vinyl tells you something in a world without streaming; owning it in 2025 mostly tells you that you like objects. In the same way, a certain typographic choice or sentence rhythm used to carry a trace of who made it; now it might just mean someone hit “regenerate” until the system lined up with mobbin or a few famous essays.

So the question becomes whether something still feels like it came by someone in particular once the shared model can do acceptable taste for almost everyone. Maybe the answer is idiosyncrasy that doesn't compress well: lives that don't show up much in the training corpus, local scenes that resist being smoothed into presets. Models will get better; that is unavoidable; the training data will get bigger, the architectures cleaner, and the post-training less clumsy. Some of the current slop is just an artefact of early instruction tuning that can be sanded away, but the basic structure stays the same. These systems are built out of past work and then installed everywhere at once.

Cultural half-life is my way of talking about how fast that system strips things of charge, and how soon an aesthetic stops feeling like a choice and starts feeling like wallpaper. Tools always change how long styles last, but what interests me here is decay driven by a single public mind that people route through for everything from ad copy to poetry. The timer on a style has gone from decades to years to months to whatever fits between two feed refreshes.
