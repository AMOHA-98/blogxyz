title: "On Cultural Half-Life: One Synthetic Mind and the Shrinking Life of Taste"

A while ago I was swapping screenshots with a designer friend. We were both poking at the new generation of model based tools. His complaint was simple: “Everything looks like a SaaS landing page from about five years ago.” Purple gradients, friendly avatars, soft cards, the usual pattern from old dribbble boards. If you gave the system very careful art direction you could drag it out of that hole, but the default kept snapping back to the same safe starter kit.

On the writing side I was seeing the same thing. Long passages that are perfectly serviceable on the surface and dead in the middle. You get a steady tempo of topic sentences and polite hedging. You get metaphors that were on slide decks at FAANG companies in 2016. You get the feeling that you are reading a composite of every high performing Medium post that has ever existed, sliced into paragraphs and reassembled in front of you.

If you work with these systems for a while you start to feel where that comes from. Pretraining takes in an absurd pile of data. Gradient descent nudges the weights until the model gets better at guessing the next token, the next pixel pattern. The easiest way to do well on that task is to sit close to the average. Lean toward phrases and layouts that appear often and rarely get punished. Then post training shows up with human feedback and safety constraints and “be helpful, be harmless, do not be weird”, and it squeezes even more of the texture out.

What you end up talking to is not a wild creative force instead you are talking to something like a centroid of our public output, polished by a bunch of corrections.

On the design side the same story plays out the web has been filling up with tasteful SaaS layout kits for more than a decade. Those kits all borrow from one another card decks, three column feature sections, mid tone gradients, the same sort of illustration where everyone is smiling and slightly off balance. Feed that into a model and you are giving it a strong signal about what “looks right” when someone says “modern product site”. The model training loop has no reason to prefer the rare layout that only appears on one weird studio’s portfolio.

We were already living in a kind of cultural remix loop before any of this. Old styles keep coming back with a knowing wink. Films reference older films. Songs borrow hooks from the back catalogue. Mark Fisher called that atmosphere hauntological, a sense that culture is walking through its own archive rather than walking toward anything new. A large model feels like that mood turned into infrastructure. It digests the public record and turns it into a thing you can query, so you end up talking to a compressed memory of what we have already done.

If you are a person who spends a lot of time around writers and designers, that begins to grate. You know how much of their taste comes from things you cannot scrape: the arguments in group chats, the years spent caring about line height, the failed drafts nobody ever posted. You also know that most users will not notice the difference, at least at first. A lot of people will look at the model made versions and shrug. It works, ship it.

That gap in reaction is where this idea of a cultural half life started for me. I kept thinking about how quickly an aesthetic now moves from someone’s thing to generic slop. Social media did some of this already. Generative models press the pedal down.

Before any of that, taste was tighter to actual people. You did not just want a designer. You wanted someone whose work you had seen and felt drawn to. You liked how they handled type on a specific poster. You liked the way they made small, slight decisions in their layouts. You knew they had gone through a path that gave them those preferences: art school, certain jobs, the years they spent obsessed with some obscure studio in a different country.

The same principle holds for writing trust usually attaches to a name. At some point you read an essay that made you stop scrolling and now you follow that person around. It is not only about the information there is a pattern in how they see the world and you choose to sign up for that pattern. They are one person, with one set of scars and jokes and blind spots. They can only write so much. If they spend a month working on something for you, that is a month they are not spending on something else.

Taste in that world means you are in contact with a limited mind. There is scarcity baked into it. Scarcity of focus, scarcity of hours, scarcity of whatever strange path produced that sensibility.

Then the feed arrived and started to play with the plumbing. Suddenly scenes that would once have stayed local showed up in your pocket. You no longer had to live in a city with a gallery to see certain artists. You did not have to know the right people in nightlife to find a micro trend. People mined niche aesthetics and spun them up into content on schedule. Content operations became a kind of factory job: pull from the archive, remix into a short clip, schedule, repeat. Faces and places changed, the pipeline stayed the same.

This did two things at once. It made aesthetics travel faster. It also introduced a different sort of gate. Algorithms decided whose work landed in your feed. If the system decided you were into a certain look, it poured more of it on you. Whole styles could exist next to one another without ever crossing feeds and people lived inside these invisible partitions and thought they were seeing the whole surface.

Even then there was friction. You could watch a video of a party and still not get through the door. You could admire a wardrobe and still not have the money for it. You could scroll through interior design accounts while sitting in a room that did not look like any of them. The gap between seeing an aesthetic and inhabiting it was still thick with practical limits.

Generative models eat some of that gap especially the part where taste is turned into media. You no longer have to spend months learning a layout tool before you put together something that looks believable. You do not have to practice copywriting for years before you ship text that sounds “professional”. You ask a system to do it and adjust the prompts until the output passes a basic sniff test.

Under the hood it is still gradient descent on a giant dataset, plus a layer of human fine tuning. Pretraining pulls the model toward the thickest clusters in the data. Post training shaves away edges that offended raters. What survives is a tone and an aesthetic sense that feels acceptable to the broadest slice of people who were involved in the training process. This is ofcourse by design a model that regularly produces deeply strange or unpleasant results would not last long in a product.

When that sort of model was locked to a research lab, it was an oddity. Now it runs inside search, office suites, slide tools, design tools, developer tools. It becomes less a novelty and more a sort of general taste server. A common mind that you can ask for diagrams, taglines, thumbnails, layouts, scripts. It will answer in the voice it has been sculpted into: helpful, steady, slightly bland.

If taste once meant “this one person, with this particular life, chose these details for you”, the new arrangement leans toward “this shared model, built from a huge pile of past work, produced something that looks close enough.” The first version comes with scarcity baked in. The second does not. When everyone can sit on top of the same synthetic author, taste shifts from being a proof that you knew a mind, into something closer to the house style of a platform.

This is where half life drops. A new visual trick appears, or a new essay cadence, and originally it belongs to the people who came up with it. They release it in public. It lands, it spreads, it starts to live in the data. Once it reaches a certain density, the model can reproduce it. Once the model can reproduce it, every marketing team under time pressure can lean on that as a preset. Whoever runs content ops in those teams does not need to hire the original minds. They need decent prompts and a sense of what will pass procurement.

An aesthetic that would once have taken years to diffuse can now be boiled down and copied in days. That copying does not require apprenticeship. There is no need to join the scene that birthed it. No need to read the same books. You are not learning the sensibility from the inside, you are renting a compressed imitation from a model API.

At that point the style starts to die. It dies not because it is bad per se, but because it has become background. Open a dozen SaaS sites and they all have the same balance of colour and copy. Open ten AI written blog posts and they all feel like they are arguing in the same register. The half life of whatever made the original feel sharp has passed. Only the author and a small group of obsessives remember that it ever felt new.

There is a word I keep using in my notes for our current culture “aesthetic grazing”. People moving through feeds at speed, snacking on micro trends without ever stopping long enough to digest any of them properly. The combination of short form video, push notifications, and low effort production makes this a natural way to live online. Generative models do not create that tendency, they turn the supply up to a setting the older content factories could not reach.

In among all of this there are still people doing careful work. There always will be. What changes is the surrounding temperature. If the baseline of acceptable writing and design is provided by a synthetic mind trained on giant corpora, then human work that sits near that baseline will vanish into it. The only things that stand out will be either much worse or much stranger.

There is a piece of strange horror fiction on Reddit called Mother Horse Eyes that has stuck with me for years (worth reading if you have time). Scattered through it is this idea of people called mixers. Their job is to sit in front of a stream of media and stitch together new combinations for an audience that has long since burned through ordinary entertainment. Old films, ads, fragments of novels, all thrown into a pot until something hits. The people consuming what they make have spent so long inside this system that nothing holds their attention for more than a moment.

When I look at the way models sit underneath content tools, I keep thinking about those mixers because there is already a version of that role appearing. People who specialise in prompting, who know how to steer image models into current fashion, who know how to get a copy that passes brand review. Their skill is not in making a single work by hand, it is in operating the machine that shapes a flood of work. The unit is not an article or a song. The unit is the pipeline.

Layer that pipeline on top of a culture already full of aesthetic grazers and you get a very specific kind of churn. Any look or tone that lands is almost immediately hauled into a content schedule. Variations get tested the better ones get boosted. The model observes those and shifts its sense of “good” a little bit more toward them. Mid Training loops back through yet another generation of work and the curve gets steeper.

If you care about taste in any serious way, this all feels a little cursed. Not in the moral panic sense more in the sense that the old signals stop working. Owning a rare piece of vinyl tells you something in a world without streaming. Owning it in 2025 mostly tells you that you like objects. In the same way, seeing a certain typographic choice or a certain sentence rhythm used to carry a trace of who made it. Now it might just mean that someone hit “regenerate” until the system lined up with mobbin and or a few famous essays.

So the question shifts if the shared model can do acceptable taste for almost everyone, what is left that still feels like it came from someone in particular. Maybe the only answer is idiosyncrasy that does not compress well. Lives that do not show up much in the training corpus. Local scenes that resist being smoothed into presets. Models will get better. That is unavoidable. The training data will get bigger, the architectures cleaner, and the post training less clumsy. Some of the current slop is just an artefact of early instruction tuning that can be sanded away. None of that alters the basic structure, which is that these systems are built out of past work and then installed everywhere at once.

Cultural half life is a way of talking about how fast that structure strips the charge from things. How soon an aesthetic stops feeling like a choice and starts feeling like default wallpaper. Some of that decay would have happened anyway. Tools always change how long styles last. The part that interests me is what happens when the decay is driven by a single public mind that people route through for everything from ad copy to poetry. It already feels like the timer on a style has gone from decades to years to months to whatever fits between two feed refreshes.
